# Doctrines, Principles & Concepts

The conceptual framework for LAWS & Military AI governance — Panchsheel,
Universal Brotherhood, Rule of Law vs Rule by Law, Just War, Responsibility
to Protect, and the IHL core principles.

<img class="img-emblem" src="/img/coat_Of_ARMS.jpg" alt="UN emblem" />

## Panchsheel — the Five Principles of Peaceful Coexistence

Signed **28 April 1954** between India and China in the wake of the Tibet
agreement, Panchsheel is the foundational diplomatic framework for South–South
relations and is consistently cited by NAM and Global South delegations.

| # | Principle | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Mutual respect for territorial integrity and sovereignty** | No infringement on borders or internal authority |
| 2 | **Mutual non-aggression** | No use or threat of force |
| 3 | **Mutual non-interference in internal affairs** | No intervention, subversion, or destabilisation |
| 4 | **Equality and mutual benefit** | Reciprocal, balanced relations |
| 5 | **Peaceful co-existence** | Settlement of disputes without recourse to war |

**LAWS relevance:** Panchsheel underpins Iran's insistence that LAWS
governance cannot be shaped by a handful of technologically dominant states.

**Official source:** Indian MEA archives [mea.gov.in](https://www.mea.gov.in/)

## Universal Brotherhood — Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam

A Sanskrit phrase from the Maha Upanishad: **"The World is One Family."**

- Invoked by India in G20 New Delhi Declaration (2023) on AI governance
- Reflected in UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI (2021)
- Increasingly cited in multilateral AI discussions as the inclusive
  alternative to Western-led frameworks

**LAWS relevance:** Provides a normative frame for inclusive, family-style
governance — Iran, India and the Global South repeatedly invoke it.

**Official sources:**
- UNESCO: [unesco.org/en/artificial-intelligence](https://www.unesco.org/en/artificial-intelligence)
- Indian MEA G20 outcomes: [g20.in](https://www.g20.in/)

## Bandung Principles (1955)

The ten principles of the Asian-African Conference (April 1955, Indonesia):
the founding political statement of the Non-Aligned Movement.

1. Respect for fundamental human rights and the Charter of the UN
2. Respect for sovereignty and territorial integrity
3. Recognition of equality of all races and nations
4. Non-intervention and non-interference
5. Mutual respect for the right of self-defence
6. Settlement of disputes by peaceful means
7. Refraining from acts or threats of aggression
8. Peaceful cooperation and mutual benefit
9. Respect for justice and international obligation
10. Promotion of world peace and cooperation

**Official source:** [cfr.org/backgrounder/bandung-conference-1955](https://www.cfr.org/backgrounder/bandung-conference-1955)

## Rule of Law vs Rule by Law

This distinction is the central normative debate in LAWS governance.

### Rule of Law

- Laws apply **equally to all**, including governments and armed forces.
- Law is **prospective, clear, public, stable**; protects individual rights.
- Independent judiciary.
- Authority is exercised **within** the law.
- Citizens and states can challenge unlawful acts.

### Rule by Law

- Government **uses** law as a tool to maintain power.
- Laws serve **the rulers**, not justice.
- Selective enforcement — applies to others but not the powerful.
- Authority is exercised **through** law (instrumental use).
- No genuine accountability.

### Application to LAWS

| Approach | What it looks like | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Rule of Law** | A universal, binding LAWS treaty with verification, applies equally to all states including P5 | Slow, hard to negotiate, may be vetoed |
| **Rule by Law** | P5 produce a political declaration or "code of conduct" that binds no one and is enforced selectively | Hollow norms, legitimises dominant states' autonomous weapons |

Iran's position: any LAWS framework that binds weaker states but exempts
NATO / Israeli / Russian programmes is **rule by law**, not rule of law.

**Official sources:**
- UN Secretary-General: [un.org/ruleoflaw](https://www.un.org/ruleoflaw/)
- ICJ: [icj-cij.org](https://www.icj-cij.org/)

## Just War Theory

Classical doctrine (Augustine, Aquinas, Grotius) and modern IHL converge on
similar criteria for legitimate use of force.

### Jus ad bellum (justice of war)

| Criterion | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Just cause** | Legitimate reason (self-defence, protecting civilians) |
| Legitimate authority | Declared by competent body |
| Right intention | To restore justice, not revenge |
| Probability of success | Not futile |
| Last resort | All peaceful options exhausted |
| Proportionality | Force proportional to threat |

### Jus in bello (justice in war)

| Criterion | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Distinction** | Combatants vs civilians must be distinguished |
| **Proportionality** | Civilian harm proportional to military advantage |
| **Military necessity** | Force necessary to achieve legitimate aim |
| Prohibition of unnecessary suffering | No weapons causing superfluous injury |

### LAWS application

LAWS challenges each criterion:
- Can a machine judge "just cause"?
- Can an algorithm assess proportionality in real time?
- Who is responsible when proportionality is breached — programmer, operator,
  commander, state?

## Responsibility to Protect (R2P)

Adopted at the **2005 UN World Summit** and endorsed by UNSC Resolution 1674
(2006). Three pillars:

1. **Every state has the responsibility to protect its population** from genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing and crimes against humanity.
2. **The international community has the responsibility to assist** states in doing so.
3. **The international community has the responsibility to take timely and decisive action** when a state manifestly fails to protect.

**LAWS relevance:** If autonomous weapons commit war crimes, R2P questions
whether the deploying state, the commander, or the manufacturer bears
responsibility.

**Official source:** [un.org/en/genocideprevention/about-responsibility-to-protect](https://www.un.org/en/genocideprevention/about-responsibility-to-protect)

## Sovereign equality

**UN Charter Article 2(1):** "The Organisation is based on the sovereign
equality of all its Members."

This is a foundational principle — but states are unequal in military
technology, economic power and diplomatic influence. LAWS negotiations
grapple with whether sovereign equality should translate into equal access
to autonomous defensive technologies or equal restraint.

**Official source:** [un.org/en/about-us/un-charter/full-text](https://www.un.org/en/about-us/un-charter/full-text)

## Self-determination

**UN Charter Article 1(2)** and **common Articles 1 of ICCPR & ICESCR**:
all peoples have the right to self-determination and to freely determine
their political status and pursue economic, social and cultural development.

**LAWS relevance:** Peoples under foreign occupation (Palestinians, Western
Sahrawis) invoke self-determination against autonomous surveillance and
targeting systems.

## Non-intervention

Codified in:
- **UN Charter Article 2(7)**: UN shall not intervene in matters essentially within domestic jurisdiction.
- **UNGA Resolution 2625 (XXV)** (1970): Friendly Relations Declaration.
- **UNGA Resolution 2131 (XX)** (1965): Declaration on the Inadmissibility of Intervention.

Iran, NAM and the Global South routinely invoke non-intervention to oppose
autonomous weapons used for extraterritorial strikes (e.g. US drone strikes
in Pakistan, Yemen, Somalia).

## Common but differentiated responsibilities (CBDR)

Origin: 1992 Rio Earth Summit. Applied to climate, now extended to AI and
emerging-technology governance.

| Dimension | Developed states | Developing states |
|---|---|---|
| Historical responsibility | High | Low |
| Capacity | High | Variable |
| Right to development | Yes | Yes — emphasised |
| Obligations | Lead by example | Pragmatic pathways |

**LAWS relevance:** Iran and the G77 argue that any LAWS framework must
recognise CBDR — developed states bear greater obligations and cannot
deny developing states access to legitimate autonomous defensive technologies.

## The IHL core principles

Codified by the ICRC and customary international law. These four principles
govern all weapons, including LAWS:

| Principle | Definition | LAWS test |
|---|---|---|
| **Distinction** | Parties must distinguish between combatants and civilians | Can an algorithm reliably distinguish a wounded combatant from a wounded civilian? |
| **Proportionality** | Incidental civilian harm must not be excessive to anticipated military advantage | Can an algorithm weigh proportionality in real time? |
| **Military necessity** | Force must be necessary to achieve a legitimate military aim | Can an algorithm assess necessity? |
| **Unnecessary suffering** | Weapons causing superfluous injury or unnecessary suffering are prohibited | Implies intrinsic limits on weapon design |

**Additional IHL principles:**
- **Precaution** in attack (Article 57 API): constant care to spare civilians.
- **No-use of prohibited weapons**: chemical, biological, blinding laser.
- **Command responsibility**: commanders responsible for subordinates' acts.

**Official sources:**
- ICRC: [icrc.org/en/war-and-law](https://www.icrc.org/en/war-and-law)
- IHL Database: [ihl-databases.icrc.org](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/)

## Precautionary principle

Origin: 1992 Rio Declaration (Principle 15): "Where there are threats of
serious or irreversible damage, lack of full scientific certainty shall not
be used as a reason for postponing cost-effective measures."

**Applied to LAWS:** Until autonomous weapons can be proven to comply with
distinction, proportionality, and precaution, the precautionary principle
argues for restraint or prohibition. This is the basis for Austria, Brazil,
and the Holy See's call for a preemptive ban.

**Official source:** [un.org/en/development/desa](https://www.un.org/en/development/desa/)

## Human control / meaningful human control

A concept developed in the CCW GGE on LAWS.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Human control** | A human exercises control over the targeting and engagement decisions |
| **Meaningful human control** | Control that is **substantive**, not nominal — sufficient information, time and authority |
| Human-in-the-loop | Human approves each engagement |
| Human-on-the-loop | Human supervises and can override |
| Human-out-of-the-loop | No human per-engagement control (the controversial case) |

Iran supports a "meaningful human control" definition but rejects
preemptive bans. Austria, Brazil, the Holy See and many NGOs argue any
human-out-of-the-loop system violates IHL.
