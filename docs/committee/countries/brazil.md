## LAWS & Military AI Stance

Brazil is the largest defence actor and technological power in Latin America, and it has approached the LAWS debate as a rising Global South power rather than as either a humanitarian-only voice or a technology-driven military power. Brasília invests actively in artificial intelligence, aerospace technology, cybersecurity, and defence innovation, including through Embraer and its defence subsidiaries, and it is determined that international regulation not consign developing countries to permanent technological dependence.

Brazil supports international regulation of autonomous weapons and has been among the more engaged GGE participants, co-authoring consensus texts and pushing for substantive discussion of definitions and human-machine interaction. Its consistent position is that fully autonomous weapons that operate without meaningful human control should be restricted, but that Brazil rejects a sweeping prohibition of all autonomy in military systems, which it sees as both impractical and potentially harmful to legitimate defensive innovation.

Brazil's diplomatic tradition emphasises multilateralism, strategic autonomy, and equitable international governance. It has argued that the LAWS debate must not be dominated by the United States, Russia, and China, and that emerging powers and developing states must have a seat at the table in shaping the rules of military AI. Brasília frequently frames the issue in terms of preventing a new technological divide in which advanced states set rules that preserve their own capabilities while constraining others.

On the definitional question, Brazil has favoured a functional approach centred on whether a system can select and engage targets without human intervention, and it has stressed the importance of predictability, reliability, and accountability across a weapon system's lifecycle. It also supports transparency measures and verification considerations, consistent with its history of nuclear safeguards cooperation with Argentina through the ABACC agreement and its late accession to the NPT in 1998.

Brazil has supported the humanitarian concerns raised by the ICRC and civil society — particularly on the risks that autonomous systems pose to distinction and proportionality — while simultaneously emphasising that developing states must retain access to dual-use technologies for economic and security purposes. This dual emphasis makes Brazil a bridge between the ban-oriented humanitarian coalition and the responsible-use camp.

## Strategic Interests

- Preventing an unregulated AI arms race while preserving Brazil's capacity to develop its own defence technology.
- Securing equitable access for developing states to AI and autonomous systems governance and technology.
- Strengthening multilateral institutions, including the CCW and the UN, and advancing its case for Security Council reform.
- Maintaining strategic autonomy from great-power competition in military AI.
- Preventing the proliferation of autonomous capabilities to non-state actors and regional destabilising forces.
- Upholding regional security and disarmament cooperation in Latin America.

## Alliances & Memberships

- **BRICS:** Coordinated position with other emerging powers seeking balanced, non-hegemonic AI governance.
- **MERCOSUR / CELAC:** Regional alignment with Argentina, Mexico, and others on humanitarian-oriented regulation.
- **G4 (with Germany, India, Japan):** Campaign for permanent Security Council representation, reinforcing Brazil's claim to a leading voice on disarmament.
- **G77+China:** Solidarity with Global South concerns about technological monopolisation.
- **CCW GGE:** One of the more active middle-power participants in drafting and consensus-building.

## UN Voting Record

Brazil voted in favour of A/RES/78/241 (2023) and A/RES/79/62 (2024) on lethal autonomous weapons systems and has endorsed the Secretary-General's consultative process and the meetings of States they initiated. In the GGE, Brazil has supported the pursuit of a legally binding outcome, while remaining publicly committed to ensuring that any instrument reflects the interests of developing states and does not simply entrench the technological lead of a few powers. Brasília is a reliable supporter of transparency, accountability, and meaningful human control language in every consensus text.

## Key Talking Points

- Regulation must prevent misuse of autonomous weapons without blocking developing countries from technological progress.
- The LAWS debate must not become a mechanism for great powers to institutionalise a military AI monopoly.
- Brazil supports a legally binding instrument that prohibits weapons lacking meaningful human control — not a ban on automation itself.
- A functional definition based on autonomous target selection and engagement is achievable and necessary.
- Accountability must span the entire lifecycle of a weapon system, from development to deployment.
- Latin America must not become a testing ground for autonomous weapons developed elsewhere.
- Brazil's ABACC and NPT history shows that responsible, equitable arms control strengthens rather than weakens development.
- Developing states need capacity-building and technology-transfer commitments, not just prohibitions.
