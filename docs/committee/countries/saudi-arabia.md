## LAWS & Military AI Stance

Saudi Arabia approaches military AI through the lens of an ambitious modernising state and one of the world's largest defence spenders. Under Vision 2030, Riyadh has made artificial intelligence and digital transformation central pillars of its economic diversification strategy, creating institutions such as the Saudi Data and AI Authority (SDAIA), a National Strategy for Data and AI, and hosting the global AI Summit in Riyadh. This investment extends to defence, where the Kingdom is modernising its armed forces, developing indigenous defence industries, and expanding the use of drones, surveillance technology, and autonomous systems.

Saudi Arabia supports the responsible development and use of AI and participates constructively in the CCW GGE on LAWS, but it generally does not favour restrictive regimes that could limit technological advancement. Like other Gulf states, Riyadh resists the framing that autonomous weapons are inherently problematic, arguing instead that governance should focus on responsible use, compliance with international humanitarian law, and prevention of misuse. The Kingdom is wary of a legally binding instrument that would entrench the military AI advantages of a few advanced powers while constraining emerging defence economies.

Riyadh's position is shaped by its security environment: it faces ballistic-missile and drone threats across the Gulf region and has invested heavily in layered air and missile defence, including systems that incorporate autonomous interception functions. Saudi officials argue that such defensive automation is essential to protecting civilian populations and infrastructure, and that prohibiting autonomous systems outright would undermine legitimate self-defence. At the same time, the Kingdom recognises the humanitarian concerns raised by fully autonomous lethal systems and supports transparency and human oversight in their use.

As a member of the Gulf Cooperation Council and the wider Islamic and Arab blocs, Saudi Arabia coordinates closely with the UAE, Kuwait, Qatar, and others in UN disarmament settings, generally aligning behind consensus language that upholds IHL, human responsibility, and the CCW process without committing to a ban. Riyadh also supports the broader Global South emphasis on equitable technology access, resisting any framework that would create a new technological divide between advanced and developing military powers.

Saudi Arabia's involvement in regional conflicts and its use of air power in Yemen have drawn scrutiny regarding the conduct of modern warfare, and the Kingdom has an interest in defending the legitimacy of precision and autonomous technologies in that context. Consequently, its delegation typically emphasises accountability, reliability, and responsible deployment rather than prohibition.

## Strategic Interests

- Protecting Vision 2030's AI and defence-modernisation agenda from restrictive international regulation.
- Building an indigenous defence-technology base, including unmanned and autonomous systems.
- Defending the legitimacy of automated air and missile defence against Gulf threats.
- Preventing a military AI advantage for regional rivals, including Iran and its proxies.
- Ensuring emerging defence economies retain equitable access to AI technologies.
- Maintaining alignment with Gulf, Arab, and Global South blocs in UN disarmament fora.

## Alliances & Memberships

- **Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC):** Coordinated Gulf positions on defence technology and disarmament diplomacy.
- **Arab League / OIC:** Bloc solidarity on technology governance and sovereignty-related language.
- **CCW GGE:** Participant supporting responsible-use principles and human oversight.
- **United States strategic partnership:** Close defence-industrial and security cooperation shaping its military AI outlook.
- **Global AI Summit / SDAIA:** Leadership in civilian AI governance, lending credibility to its responsible-use framing.

## UN Voting Record

Saudi Arabia voted in favour of A/RES/78/241 (2023) and A/RES/79/62 (2024) on lethal autonomous weapons systems and has supported the CCW process and the General Assembly's consultative steps. In the GGE, Riyadh backs consensus principles on human responsibility, IHL applicability, and accountability, while resisting proposals for a comprehensive legally binding ban. The Kingdom generally votes with the majority consensus on LAWS resolutions and uses its statements to emphasise responsible use, sovereignty, and the legitimate defensive applications of automation.

## Key Talking Points

- Saudi Arabia supports responsible AI and has made it a cornerstone of Vision 2030 — governance should enable innovation, not block it.
- Automated air and missile defence has protected civilian lives and critical infrastructure in the Gulf.
- International humanitarian law already governs autonomous systems; the task is responsible implementation, not prohibition.
- A ban would entrench the military AI advantages of a few states at the expense of emerging defence economies.
- Human oversight and accountability are essential and should be codified as principles of responsible use.
- Developing states must have equitable access to AI and autonomous technologies.
- The CCW is the right forum; Riyadh supports consensus-building there.
- Governance should focus on misuse, reliability, and safeguards rather than the technology itself.
