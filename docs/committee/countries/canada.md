## LAWS & Military AI Stance

Canada, although geographically located in North America, is consistently considered within the Western alliance perspective due to its NATO membership and close security cooperation with European partners. Ottawa supports responsible military AI development while emphasizing compliance with international humanitarian law. Canada has participated actively in international discussions on autonomous weapons and has generally supported transparency, accountability, and meaningful human involvement in decisions involving the use of force.

Canadian policy draws on a tradition of active engagement in disarmament and arms control, from leadership on landmine and cluster-munition treaties to consistent support for humanitarian disarmament norms. In the LAWS debate, Ottawa has argued that human beings must remain responsible for lethal decisions and that autonomous weapons raising serious concerns under international humanitarian law should not be developed or used. Canada's emphasis on human control, transparency, and testing has made it a constructive, moderate voice in the CCW Group of Governmental Experts (GGE) on LAWS.

In its recent defence policy, Canada has affirmed the importance of meaningful human control over lethal autonomous systems and committed to responsible, law-abiding development of AI-enabled capabilities. Ottawa sees military AI as valuable for the Canadian Armed Forces in domains such as surveillance, intelligence, cyber defence, logistics, and operations in the Arctic — a region of growing strategic significance where automation and sensing play an expanding role. Canada seeks to use these technologies within clear legal and ethical boundaries.

Within NATO, NORAD, and the Five Eyes intelligence alliance, Canada works on interoperable AI and autonomous systems with allied partners while pushing for shared principles of responsibility, lawfulness, and accountability. Its position therefore resembles that of the Netherlands and Germany in supporting strong norms and human control, while remaining mindful of alliance interoperability and the need to maintain democratic technological advantage. Canada's recent term on the Security Council (2021–2022) reinforced its engagement with emerging technologies in international peace and security.

## Strategic Interests

- Maintaining human control and accountability in military AI while preserving allied interoperability, including with the United States through NORAD.
- Supporting the development of international norms and legally meaningful rules on autonomous weapons in the CCW and the General Assembly.
- Protecting Arctic sovereignty through AI-enabled surveillance and sensing while keeping such systems within legal and ethical bounds.
- Upholding Canada's humanitarian disarmament legacy in the LAWS debate.
- Ensuring Canadian and allied defence industries can innovate responsibly in AI, robotics, and unmanned systems.
- Strengthening the rules-based international order as a middle power with strong multilateral engagement.

## Alliances & Memberships

- **NATO** — Supports the alliance's Artificial Intelligence Strategy and its emphasis on responsibility, lawfulness, and human accountability.
- **NORAD** — Cooperates with the United States on air and missile defence, where AI-enabled sensing and decision support are increasingly central.
- **Five Eyes** — Engages in intelligence-sharing and technology coordination with close partners.
- **CCW / GGE on LAWS** — Active participant advocating transparency, testing, and human control.
- **G7** — Contributes to global AI governance discussions, including responsible development frameworks.

## UN Voting Record

Canada has voted in favour of the General Assembly resolutions on lethal autonomous weapons systems, including A/RES/78/241 (2023) and A/RES/79/62 (2024), and has supported related language on human control and international humanitarian law in the First Committee. Ottawa generally aligns with like-minded Western and humanitarian-aligned states, backing sustained GGE work and openness to a legally binding instrument while remaining attentive to alliance interoperability. Canada's record reflects a consistent middle position: strong on norms and accountability, pragmatic on responsible innovation and allied technological advantage.

## Key Talking Points

- Human beings must always remain responsible and in control of decisions involving lethal force.
- Compliance with international humanitarian law — distinction, proportionality, and precaution — is non-negotiable for AI-enabled weapons.
- Transparency, testing, and verification requirements should be agreed to prevent unpredictable or indiscriminate systems.
- Canada's humanitarian disarmament record shows that strong norms and responsible innovation can advance together.
- Responsible military AI strengthens deterrence and defence in domains like Arctic surveillance, cyber, and logistics.
- Rules must be universal and preserve democratic technological advantage; unchecked autonomy would destabilize everyone.
- Middle powers like Canada play a critical bridging role between military powers, humanitarian states, and civil society.
