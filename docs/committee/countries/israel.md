## LAWS & Military AI Stance

Israel is one of the world's most advanced developers of autonomous and AI-enabled defence systems and therefore approaches the LAWS debate primarily as a producing state that operates in an acute threat environment. Its security doctrine, shaped by persistent regional tensions, asymmetric conflict, and sophisticated missile and rocket threats, has driven heavy investment in drones, loitering munitions, missile defence, AI-assisted intelligence and targeting, and active protection systems. Israel regards these technologies as life-saving defensive capabilities that reduce risks to soldiers and civilians alike.

Israel opposes legally binding international restrictions that would constrain military innovation. It argues that international humanitarian law already provides sufficient standards — including distinction, proportionality, and precaution — and that the appropriate focus is responsible deployment and human oversight rather than prohibition. Israeli defence companies, including IAI, Rafael, and Elbit Systems, produce systems such as Iron Dome, David's Sling, Arrow, the Trophy active protection system, and the Harpy and Harop loitering munitions, which are deployed and exported worldwide, making Israel acutely sensitive to any regulatory regime that would restrict its defence-industrial base.

At the same time, Israel acknowledges the importance of human involvement. Its delegation in the GGE has stressed that humans must remain responsible and accountable for the use of force, that autonomous systems require robust testing and reliability, and that the human-machine relationship must remain under command authority. Israel's reported use of AI-assisted targeting tools in military operations, including systems described as "Lavender" and "The Gospel", has drawn international scrutiny, and Israel publicly maintains that such tools support — rather than replace — human decision-making.

Israel endorses the approach of the United States-led Political Declaration on Responsible Military Use of Artificial Intelligence and Autonomy, which emphasises responsible use, legal compliance, and human responsibility while explicitly rejecting an arms-control treaty on AI. Israel has positioned itself firmly in the responsible-use camp, arguing that the principal risk is not autonomy itself but irresponsible implementation, and that well-designed autonomous systems can improve compliance with international humanitarian law through faster, more precise targeting.

Israel's central argument is that a state facing existential threats cannot be expected to forfeit the defensive advantages of automation while adversaries continue to develop such capabilities. It frames the LAWS debate as a tension between idealised humanitarian norms and the hard realities of national security, and it calls on the committee to recognise that regulation must not benefit states that already possess overwhelming conventional and strategic superiority.

## Strategic Interests

- Preserving freedom to develop, procure, and export advanced autonomous and AI-enabled defence systems.
- Defending Israel's qualitative military edge in a region of persistent asymmetric threats.
- Ensuring IHL compliance is framed as a matter of responsible use rather than prohibition.
- Preventing adversaries and non-state actors — including Iran and its proxies — from acquiring comparable autonomous capabilities.
- Maintaining close alignment with the United States on military AI governance.
- Countering what Israel views as one-sided criticism of its targeting practices in UN fora.

## Alliances & Memberships

- **United States:** Principal ally and co-endorser of the Political Declaration on Responsible Military Use of AI; shared opposition to binding AI treaties.
- **CCW GGE:** Active participant defending the responsible-use position and human-oversight framing.
- **Abraham Accords (UAE, Bahrain, Morocco, Sudan):** Normalisation frameworks that open regional technology and defence cooperation channels.
- **Export relationships (NATO states, India, and others):** Extensive markets for Israeli autonomous systems that a binding instrument could disrupt.

## UN Voting Record

Israel voted in favour of A/RES/78/241 (2023) and A/RES/79/62 (2024) on lethal autonomous weapons systems, consistent with its willingness to engage procedurally, but it has consistently cautioned in the First Committee and the GGE against negotiating a legally binding instrument. Israel supports consensus principles, human responsibility, and IHL compliance, and it has endorsed the Political Declaration on Responsible Military AI, but it rejects calls for a ban or a new treaty, arguing that existing law and responsible-use frameworks are sufficient. Israel also frequently abstains or votes against language it perceives as singling out specific states or pre-judging the legitimacy of autonomous systems.

## Key Talking Points

- Autonomous systems save lives — Iron Dome has intercepted thousands of rockets, and AI-assisted targeting can reduce civilian harm.
- International humanitarian law already governs autonomous weapons; what is needed is responsible implementation, not a new treaty.
- Israel fully supports human responsibility and accountability; machines support commanders, they do not replace them.
- A binding ban would disarm states facing existential threats while leaving adversaries free to develop such capabilities.
- Well-tested autonomous systems can comply with distinction and proportionality better than humans under extreme time pressure.
- The debate must not be distorted by politicised criticism of Israel's operations in UN fora.
- Israel supports the Political Declaration on Responsible Military AI and urges universal endorsement of its principles.
- Regulation should focus on misuse, reliability, and safeguards rather than on the technology itself.
