## LAWS & Military AI Stance

India is among the most influential voices from the Global South and the Indo-Pacific in the debate on lethal autonomous weapons systems. New Delhi treats artificial intelligence as a transformative technology with direct consequences for national security, and the Indian Armed Forces have begun integrating AI into intelligence analysis, autonomous surveillance, border monitoring, cybersecurity, predictive maintenance, and unmanned systems. Institutional commitments such as the Defence Artificial Intelligence Council (DAIC) and the Defence AI Project Agency (DAIPA), established under the Ministry of Defence, signal India's ambition to embed AI across its defence ecosystem.

India has not supported a complete prohibition of LAWS. Instead, New Delhi argues for balanced international regulation that recognizes both security requirements and humanitarian concerns. Indian diplomacy consistently frames the discussion around responsible use, accountability, and transparency rather than halting technological development altogether.

India's position is deeply shaped by its strategic environment. Facing security challenges along its land borders with China and Pakistan and across the Indian Ocean, New Delhi considers advanced military technologies essential for maintaining deterrence and operational effectiveness. Autonomous systems, AI-enabled surveillance, and unmanned platforms are viewed as valuable tools for operating in difficult terrain such as the Himalayas and for strengthening maritime domain awareness.

At the same time, India insists that machines should not independently determine the use of lethal force. New Delhi emphasizes human responsibility in warfare and supports the continuation of international discussions under the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW) and its Group of Governmental Experts (GGE). However, as a CCW State Party that is also a nuclear-armed non-NPT party, India is wary of restrictive treaty commitments that could constrain its national security capabilities.

India's approach therefore occupies a middle position between technologically advanced states seeking flexibility and humanitarian coalitions demanding strong restrictions. It advocates responsible innovation while preserving strategic autonomy, positioning itself as a bridge between the Global North and the developing world on emerging weapons technologies.

## Strategic Interests

- Preserving strategic autonomy and freedom to develop AI-enabled defence capabilities without binding treaty constraints.
- Maintaining conventional deterrence and battlefield effectiveness vis-à-vis China and Pakistan, including in high-altitude and maritime environments.
- Strengthening border surveillance and maritime domain awareness across the Indian Ocean region.
- Positioning India as a leading Global South voice in disarmament forums and emerging-technology governance.
- Preventing international frameworks from creating technological inequality or enshrining major-power monopolies over AI.
- Countering the proliferation of autonomous capabilities to non-state actors and terrorist groups.
- Protecting access to dual-use AI technology, data, and computing infrastructure essential to national development.

## Alliances & Memberships

- Quad (Quadrilateral Security Dialogue): partnership with the United States, Japan, and Australia with growing emphasis on secure and responsible technology cooperation.
- Non-Aligned Movement (NAM): underpins India's principled, non-bloc stance and Global South credibility in arms control debates.
- BRICS: forum for cooperation with China, Russia, Brazil, and South Africa, including discussions on technology governance.
- Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO): regional platform where India engages China and Pakistan on security and confidence-building measures.
- Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW): State Party and active participant in the GGE on LAWS.

## UN Voting Record

India has voted in favour of the consensus resolutions on lethal autonomous weapons systems adopted in the General Assembly First Committee, including A/RES/78/241 (2023) and A/RES/79/62 (2024), which reaffirm the CCW GGE mandate and underscore human responsibility and accountability. However, India has consistently avoided committing to language that would oblige the immediate negotiation of a legally binding instrument and has resisted proposals for an outright ban.

In GGE talks, New Delhi advocates pragmatic, incremental outcomes — political principles, transparency measures, and confidence-building — rather than a rushed treaty. Its posture is calibrated to preserve national flexibility while demonstrating support for responsible international governance, and it emphasizes that any framework must respect the right of developing states to modernize their defence capabilities.

## Key Talking Points

- "India supports responsible use of emerging technologies, not a blanket prohibition that ignores legitimate security needs."
- "Autonomous systems can strengthen border surveillance and maritime security; regulation must not deny states lawful defensive tools."
- "Any instrument must retain meaningful human control and clear accountability for the use of lethal force."
- "Weapons regulation must not create a technological divide between developed and developing states."
- "Existing international humanitarian law applies to all weapons; the CCW GGE is the right forum for gradual progress."
- "India is a State Party to the CCW and remains committed to constructive multilateral dialogue on LAWS."
- "Transparency, confidence-building measures, and verification must accompany any future framework."
- "As a nuclear-armed state, India understands strategic stability; hasty treaties risk destabilizing regional balances."
