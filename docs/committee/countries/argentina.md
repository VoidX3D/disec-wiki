## LAWS & Military AI Stance

Argentina approaches the LAWS debate from a tradition of arms control, international humanitarian law, and peaceful dispute resolution. As a founding member of the United Nations and a party to the NPT, the Chemical Weapons Convention, the Biological Weapons Convention, and the Ottawa Convention banning anti-personnel mines, Buenos Aires has consistently placed humanitarian considerations at the centre of its disarmament diplomacy. It views lethal autonomous weapons through this same lens, arguing that emerging technologies must not erode the protection of civilians in armed conflict or outpace the international legal framework that governs the use of force.

Argentina supports a regulatory outcome anchored in meaningful human control. It argues that decisions involving lethal force must remain traceable to human judgment and accountability, and that no weapon system should be permitted to select and engage targets entirely without human supervision. In the CCW Group of Governmental Experts (GGE) on LAWS, Argentina has engaged constructively on definitional questions, stressing that international humanitarian law, including the principles of distinction, proportionality, and precaution, applies fully to autonomous and AI-enabled weapons.

While Argentina backs stronger international regulation, it does not champion an unconditional ban on all autonomous technologies. Its position is humanitarian-leaning but pragmatic: it favours a legally binding instrument that would prohibit weapons operating without meaningful human control while preserving the legitimate, defensive uses of automation in military systems. Buenos Aires also warns against permitting a small group of technologically advanced states to define the rules of military AI at the expense of the developing world, mirroring a concern shared across Latin America about technological inequality.

Argentine diplomacy on this file is closely coordinated with its regional partners, particularly Brazil and Mexico. The three states have often aligned in first-committee voting and in the GGE on questions of transparency, accountability, and preventing an unregulated AI arms race in the hemisphere. Argentina also draws on its own history of constructive nuclear confidence-building with Brazil in the 1990s — including the creation of ABACC — as evidence that regional cooperation can reduce the risks of advanced weapons proliferation.

Although Argentina is not a leading developer of autonomous weapons, its defence sector has modernised with increased use of drones, surveillance technology, and cyber capabilities for border control and security in its Andean and maritime frontier regions. This gives Buenos Aires a stake in ensuring that regulation addresses the proliferation of inexpensive autonomous systems to non-state actors and destabilising actors, a theme it has stressed in UN discussions.

## Strategic Interests

- Preventing the emergence of an unregulated AI weapons arms race that could destabilise Latin America.
- Maintaining meaningful human control and accountability in all use-of-force decisions.
- Ensuring that Global South states are not excluded from military AI governance or from equitable access to emerging technologies.
- Containing the proliferation of low-cost autonomous and unmanned systems to non-state actors in the region.
- Reinforcing its traditional identity as a responsible, humanitarian-oriented disarmament actor.
- Strengthening multilateral fora — CCW, GGE, and the General Assembly First Committee — as the legitimate venue for LAWS governance.

## Alliances & Memberships

- **MERCOSUR / CELAC:** Regional bloc coordination on disarmament positions and shared Latin American regulatory concerns.
- **Organization of American States (OAS):** Hemisphere-wide security dialogue on emerging threats and technological governance.
- **G77+China / Non-Aligned Movement:** Alignment with Global South positions on equitable technology access and against technological monopolisation.
- **G20:** Participation in broader debates on the economic and security dimensions of artificial intelligence.
- **UN First Committee / CCW GGE:** Argentina's primary multilateral arena for advancing its LAWS position.

## UN Voting Record

Argentina voted in favour of the landmark General Assembly resolutions on lethal autonomous weapons systems — A/RES/78/241 (December 2023) and A/RES/79/62 (December 2024) — both of which were adopted by overwhelming majorities (164-3-12 and 165-5-11 respectively), and Buenos Aires supports the resulting Secretary-General's consultations and meetings of States on the issue. It has consistently endorsed the work of the CCW GGE and the call for States to pursue a legally binding instrument. Argentina is not a champion of the most maximalist ban position, but it reliably supports the humanitarian-oriented coalition of Latin American states pressing for stronger legal commitments on meaningful human control.

## Key Talking Points

- No machine should be given the authority to decide who lives and who dies; meaningful human control is non-negotiable.
- The absence of a common definition of LAWS is not a reason to delay — Argentina urges the GGE to agree on core elements of a definition.
- Existing international humanitarian law applies to autonomous weapons, but a legally binding instrument is needed to clarify its application.
- Regulation must not become a cover for technological monopolisation by a few advanced military powers.
- The proliferation of inexpensive drones and autonomous systems to non-state actors is a direct threat to states like Argentina.
- Transparency and accountability mechanisms, including reliable human-machine interaction in targeting, are essential.
- Latin American states have a shared interest in preventing a destabilising AI arms race in the hemisphere.
- Argentina supports the CCW as the appropriate negotiating forum and urges progress toward an outcome document with binding force.
