## LAWS & Military AI Stance

Mexico is among the most humanitarian-oriented states in the global debate over autonomous weapons and has consistently been one of the strongest voices calling for a legally binding instrument that would prohibit lethal autonomous weapons operating without meaningful human control. Mexico's disarmament identity is anchored in a long history of nuclear advocacy: it was one of the first states to ratify the NPT, the driving force behind the 1967 Treaty of Tlatelolco establishing the Latin American nuclear-weapon-free zone, and the first state in the world to sign and ratify the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons (TPNW) in 2018.

Mexico applies this same humanitarian logic to LAWS. It argues that machines must never be permitted to independently decide to use lethal force, and that the principle of human dignity requires that life-and-death decisions remain with accountable human beings. In the CCW GGE, Mexico has consistently supported the positions of the ban-oriented coalition, joining Austria and other like-minded states in pressing for negotiated prohibitions on weapons that select and engage targets without human intervention.

Mexico emphasises that existing international humanitarian law is not sufficient on its own to govern autonomous weapons, because the pace of technological development has outstripped the law. It therefore calls for a new, legally binding instrument — ideally a protocol under the CCW — that would codify prohibitions and constraints, establish definitions, and require human control as a matter of law rather than policy. Mexico is also a strong proponent of transparency, national implementation measures, and post-deployment accountability.

As a non-possessor and non-developer of autonomous weapons, Mexico approaches the debate from the perspective of a potential victim state. It warns that inexpensive autonomous systems could proliferate to criminal and non-state actors in Latin America, where Mexico faces significant challenges from organised crime, and it insists that global rules must protect states that cannot match the military AI capabilities of major powers.

Mexico's leadership in the humanitarian disarmament "open cities" and stockpiling dialogues for nuclear weapons has carried over to the LAWS file, where it co-sponsors resolutions, participates actively in GGE sessions, and frequently aligns with civil society and ICRC recommendations. Mexico views the LAWS agenda as part of a broader normative project to ensure that emerging technology serves human security rather than undermining it.

## Strategic Interests

- Securing a legally binding instrument that prohibits fully autonomous lethal weapons lacking meaningful human control.
- Preventing a technological divide in which weaker states become more vulnerable to powerful military AI states.
- Guarding against proliferation of autonomous systems to non-state actors, including organised crime.
- Upholding Mexico's role as a global leader in humanitarian disarmament diplomacy.
- Ensuring human dignity and human rights remain central to the governance of military AI.
- Strengthening the CCW and the UN First Committee as effective normative venues.

## Alliances & Memberships

- **Treaty of Tlatelolco / OPANAL:** Regional nuclear-weapon-free-zone framework reflecting Mexico's arms-control identity.
- **Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons (TPNW):** First signatory and ratifier; reinforcing humanitarian disarmament leadership.
- **CCW GGE:** Active participant in the ban-oriented coalition within the LAWS negotiations.
- **G77+China / CELAC:** Coordination with Global South states on equitable technology governance.
- **Like-minded group (with Austria, Ireland, and others):** Coalition pushing for a legally binding LAWS instrument.

## UN Voting Record

Mexico voted in favour of A/RES/78/241 (2023) and A/RES/79/62 (2024) and is a leading co-sponsor of LAWS-related resolutions in the First Committee. Mexico consistently argues that the GGE should move from consensus principles to a negotiating mandate for a legally binding instrument. It has supported every procedural step requested by the General Assembly — including the Secretary-General's report and the meetings of States — and has pressed for the participation of developing countries and civil society in those processes. Mexico's posture is unambiguously aligned with the humanitarian coalition favouring prohibition over permissive regulation.

## Key Talking Points

- Machines must never be allowed to decide to take a human life; meaningful human control is a matter of human dignity.
- International humanitarian law applies, but it is not enough — the law must be codified in a binding instrument before technology races ahead.
- Mexico, as the first TPNW signatory, calls on states to show equal ambition on autonomous weapons.
- Proliferation of autonomous systems to criminal and non-state actors is a real and immediate danger in Latin America.
- A CCW protocol prohibiting weapons without meaningful human control is the most realistic binding path forward.
- States developing LAWS must accept transparency and accountability for the full lifecycle of their systems.
- Smaller and developing states cannot be expected to defend themselves against autonomous systems they cannot afford or regulate.
- Mexico urges the GGE to move from principles to negotiation without further delay.
