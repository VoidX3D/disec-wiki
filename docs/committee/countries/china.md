## LAWS & Military AI Stance

China participates actively in CCW and Group of Governmental Experts (GGE) discussions on LAWS and presents itself as a supporter of responsible international governance, but it resists legally binding restrictions that could constrain its military modernization. Beijing has proposed principles emphasizing responsible use and the prevention of misuse, arguing that international governance should concentrate on how autonomous weapons are used rather than on restricting research and development. This distinction allows China to project a constructive posture while protecting its technological freedom of action.

Beijing treats artificial intelligence as a strategic technology central to national power. The 2017 New Generation Artificial Intelligence Development Plan set the ambition of global AI leadership by 2030, and Chinese military doctrine has embraced the concept of "intelligentized warfare" (智能化战争), in which AI, robotics, information systems, and autonomous platforms are integrated into every layer of military operations. China's large technology sector, extensive data resources, and state-supported AI programmes provide significant advantages in this competition.

Like Russia, China emphasizes sovereignty and strategic independence. It is cautious about accepting internationally negotiated standards that could be shaped by Western powers to disadvantage Chinese development, and it opposes frameworks it views as extraterritorial or as disguised export-control regimes. At the same time, Beijing uses humanitarian language in diplomatic forums, supporting concepts such as meaningful human control in principle while resisting definitions and binding commitments that would freeze evolving technology.

China has repeatedly cautioned against an AI arms race and called for the prevention of an uncontrolled militarization of artificial intelligence, yet it continues to invest heavily in autonomous drones, AI-assisted intelligence systems, and cyber capabilities, including in the Taiwan Strait and South China Sea operational environments. Its diplomatic strategy balances calls for confidence-building with the preservation of maximum freedom to develop military AI.

In the GGE, China has supported the continuation of dialogue and has proposed guidelines on responsible behaviour rather than endorsing a prohibitive treaty. Beijing's position reflects its interest in preventing U.S.-led norm-setting that could legitimize Western technological dominance while ensuring that any eventual international instrument does not impede Chinese military AI.

## Strategic Interests

- Maintain technological sovereignty and freedom of action in AI, robotics, and autonomous systems.
- Prevent international rules from legitimizing Western export controls or extraterritorial standards.
- Avoid being disadvantaged in any AI arms race with the United States.
- Secure a seat at the table in any norm-setting process to shape outcomes favourable to Chinese interests.
- Counter potential autonomous threats in the Taiwan Strait, South China Sea, and along contested borders.
- Preserve the CCW consensus process as a brake on treaty-oriented negotiations.

## Alliances & Memberships

- **Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO)** — forum for security cooperation and common positions with Russia and Central Asian states.
- **BRICS** — platform for coordinating Global South and non-Western approaches to emerging technology governance.
- **China–Russia Comprehensive Strategic Partnership** — shared resistance to binding LAWS restrictions and alignment on sovereignty-based positions.
- **CCW GGE on LAWS** — active participant promoting guidelines and responsible-use principles rather than prohibition.

## UN Voting Record

China has joined consensus on the annual UN General Assembly resolutions on lethal autonomous weapons systems, from A/RES/73/32 through A/RES/78/241 (2023) and A/RES/79/62 (2024), supporting the continuation of the GGE process. In the GGE, China has submitted working papers and proposals for guiding principles on the responsible use of AI in the military domain, including calls for the prevention of an AI arms race and for confidence-building measures. Beijing consistently resists treaty-oriented outcomes, arguing that premature binding regulation is premature without definitional consensus and would limit legitimate national development. It positions itself as a bridge between the Global South and the major powers, seeking influence over the eventual shape of international rules.

## Key Talking Points

- Military AI development is unavoidable; governance should target misuse, not restrict legitimate research and development.
- State sovereignty must be respected; no state should impose extraterritorial standards on another's AI programmes.
- Existing international humanitarian law applies to all weapons; a new treaty is unnecessary and would be unverifiable.
- Without definitional consensus, a binding instrument would be premature and risk freezing technology at an arbitrary point.
- The world must prevent an AI arms race, and calls for restraint must apply equally to all states.
- Reject Western unilateral norms and export-control measures that function as disguised restrictions on developing states.
- Support meaningful human control in principle, but oppose definitions that would capture benign automation and dual-use technology.
- Confidence-building measures and transparency among states should precede any legal commitments.
