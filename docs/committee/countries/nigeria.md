## LAWS & Military AI Stance

Nigeria approaches the LAWS debate from the perspective of Africa's largest economy and most populous country, whose security policy is dominated by counterterrorism, internal security, and counterinsurgency operations against groups such as Boko Haram and Islamic State West Africa Province. These challenges have driven Nigeria to use surveillance technologies, unmanned aerial systems, and digital tools for security purposes, and Abuja recognises the value of advanced technology in protecting its population and territory.

At the same time, Nigeria remains cautious about fully autonomous lethal systems. Its priorities are preventing the proliferation of autonomous weapons to non-state actors, strengthening cybersecurity, and ensuring that technological development supports peace and stability rather than instability. Nigeria is particularly concerned that inexpensive autonomous and drone systems could fall into the hands of terrorist and insurgent groups operating in the Sahel and Lake Chad regions, where state capacity is already stretched.

Nigeria supports international oversight of autonomous weapons and has engaged constructively in the CCW GGE and UN First Committee debates, aligning with the broader African and Global South position that emphasises meaningful human control, accountability, and the prevention of a new technological divide. Abuja argues that states with limited military AI capabilities must not be left defenceless as autonomous weapons proliferate, and that international rules must protect weaker states from destabilisation.

As a state with ambitions to industrialise and develop its own defence-industrial base, Nigeria resists frameworks that would lock developing countries out of access to emerging technology. Its position is therefore balanced: it supports stronger international regulation and meaningful human control, while insisting that legitimate defensive uses of AI and autonomous systems remain accessible to African states.

Nigeria also brings a strong peacekeeping and regional-security perspective to the debate. As a major troop contributor to UN and ECOWAS peace operations and a long-serving voice for African interests in the UN, Abuja stresses the importance of protecting peacekeepers and civilians from the dangers of autonomous warfare, and it advocates for African representation in all global AI governance processes.

## Strategic Interests

- Preventing autonomous weapons and cheap drone systems from reaching terrorist and non-state actors.
- Maintaining access to surveillance and defence technology for counterterrorism and internal security.
- Preventing a new technological divide that leaves African states more vulnerable.
- Ensuring meaningful human control and accountability in all use-of-force decisions.
- Strengthening cybersecurity capacity in the face of AI-enabled threats.
- Advancing African representation and interests in global AI governance.

## Alliances & Memberships

- **African Union / ECOWAS:** Regional coordination on security technology, counterterrorism, and disarmament positions.
- **CCW GGE:** Participant supporting oversight-oriented regulation.
- **G77+China / D-8 / OIC:** Solidarity with Global South and Islamic blocs on equitable technology access.
- **United Nations First Committee:** Active African voice in disarmament debates.
- **Commonwealth:** Additional forum for coordinating positions on emerging technologies.

## UN Voting Record

Nigeria voted in favour of A/RES/78/241 (2023) and A/RES/79/62 (2024) on lethal autonomous weapons systems and has supported the General Assembly's consultative process and meetings of States. In the GGE and First Committee, Abuja consistently backs language on meaningful human control, IHL compliance, and accountability, and it supports moving toward a legally binding instrument while insisting that African perspectives and capacity-building needs are reflected. Nigeria generally votes with the humanitarian-leaning majority of the Global South on LAWS resolutions.

## Key Talking Points

- Autonomous weapons must never be allowed to fall into the hands of terrorist and non-state actors.
- Nigeria's counterterrorism experience shows the danger of proliferating cheap drone and autonomous technology.
- African states cannot be expected to defend themselves against autonomous weapons they cannot regulate or afford.
- Meaningful human control and accountability must be guaranteed by international law.
- Governance must include African states, not be decided by a few technological powers.
- Cybersecurity is part of the military AI problem and must be part of the solution.
- Nigeria supports responsible access to surveillance and defence technology for legitimate security needs.
- As an elected Security Council member for 2026–2027, Nigeria will champion African and Global South interests on LAWS.
