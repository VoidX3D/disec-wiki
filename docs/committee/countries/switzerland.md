## LAWS & Military AI Stance

Switzerland represents one of the strongest supporters of humanitarian regulation of autonomous weapons, owing to its neutrality, diplomatic traditions, and historical role in the development of international humanitarian law. As the host country of the International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC) and of the United Nations' European headquarters, Switzerland has played an important role in discussions concerning the humanitarian implications of autonomous weapons. Its engagement reflects a long line of Swiss humanitarian diplomacy, from the Geneva Conventions to humanitarian disarmament initiatives.

Swiss policy emphasizes legal clarity, accountability, and the preservation of human responsibility in decisions involving lethal force. Bern argues that autonomy in weapons systems creates serious risks to the protection of civilians, the ability to comply with the principles of distinction and proportionality, and the effective functioning of international humanitarian law. Switzerland stresses that human beings must remain capable of controlling and overriding autonomous systems and that responsibility for the use of force cannot be transferred to machines.

Switzerland participates actively and constructively in the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW) and the Group of Governmental Experts (GGE) on LAWS, working closely with the ICRC and humanitarian-aligned states. It supports the negotiation of robust international rules — including legally binding elements where feasible — while recognizing legitimate defensive uses of autonomy. Bern consistently advocates concrete measures such as prohibitions on unpredictable systems, human control requirements, and life-cycle accountability, testing, and transparency standards.

Switzerland's position is reinforced by its recent experience as a non-permanent member of the Security Council (2023–2024), during which it worked on AI and emerging technologies in the context of international peace and security. Though not a member of NATO or the EU, Switzerland coordinates closely with like-minded European states and the ICRC, giving it a distinct bridge-building role between Western, humanitarian, and neutral perspectives in disarmament diplomacy. Its national AI strategy applies ethical and human-centred principles, and Bern champions these values in military applications as well.

## Strategic Interests

- Preserving Switzerland's role as a global hub for humanitarian law and disarmament diplomacy in Geneva.
- Ensuring that autonomous weapons do not undermine the protection of civilians or the credibility of international humanitarian law.
- Supporting negotiations that produce legally binding rules, human control, and accountability alongside practical transparency and testing standards.
- Leveraging neutrality to build bridges between NATO, EU, non-aligned, and civil society positions in the LAWS debate.
- Preventing the proliferation and misuse of unpredictable autonomous systems while allowing responsible defensive applications.
- Upholding Swiss credibility as host of the ICRC and of major UN bodies.

## Alliances & Memberships

- **CCW / GGE on LAWS** — A leading voice in Geneva for humanitarian regulation and legally binding measures.
- **ICRC / Geneva Conventions system** — As host country, Switzerland closely aligns its diplomacy with ICRC legal and humanitarian expertise.
- **OSCE** — Engages in European security and confidence-building without NATO or EU membership.
- **Council of Europe** — Contributes to human-rights and rule-of-law frameworks relevant to AI governance.
- **Non-permanent UN Security Council (2023–2024)** — Recently applied its bridge-building approach to AI and peace and security debates.

## UN Voting Record

Switzerland has voted in favour of the General Assembly resolutions on lethal autonomous weapons systems, including A/RES/78/241 (2023) and A/RES/79/62 (2024), and supported their adoption during its Security Council tenure as well. In the First Committee, Bern consistently backs strong language on meaningful human control, compliance with international humanitarian law, and the negotiation of legally binding measures. Switzerland's voting record, together with its Geneva-based humanitarian leadership, places it among the most regulation-oriented Western states in the LAWS debate.

## Key Talking Points

- As host of the ICRC, Switzerland insists that humanitarian law must be the foundation of any regulation of autonomous weapons.
- Humans must always retain control over and the ability to override autonomous systems; responsibility cannot be delegated to machines.
- Unpredictable autonomous systems create unacceptable risks for civilians and for the credibility of international humanitarian law.
- The GGE should move toward legally binding rules, including prohibitions, human control requirements, and accountability standards.
- Transparency, testing, and verification are essential complements to any negotiated instrument.
- Neutrality allows Switzerland to build consensus between NATO, EU, non-aligned, and civil society positions.
- States must adopt domestic measures reflecting international commitments, as Swiss institutions already do.
