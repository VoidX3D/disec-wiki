## LAWS & Military AI Stance

The United States approaches lethal autonomous weapons systems and military AI primarily through national defence policy rather than international prohibition. Its governing instrument is Department of Defense Directive 3000.09, first issued in 2012 and revised in 2023, which requires appropriate levels of human judgment over the use of force, rigorous testing, legal review, and responsible command structures for autonomous and semi-autonomous weapons. Washington argues these internal safeguards demonstrate a credible commitment to responsible military AI while preserving freedom to innovate.

Washington consistently opposes a legally binding international treaty that would prohibit autonomous weapons. It maintains that international humanitarian law is technology-neutral, already applies to all weapons systems including autonomous ones, and therefore does not require a new instrument. The United States warns that a comprehensive ban would prevent beneficial defensive applications, disadvantage law-abiding states against adversaries who ignore the ban, and freeze emerging capabilities with legitimate humanitarian value, such as autonomous mine clearance, terminal defence, and precision strike systems that reduce civilian harm.

Instead, Washington champions a responsible-use framework. In 2023 the United States introduced and circulated a Political Declaration on Responsible Military Use of Artificial Intelligence and Autonomy, subsequently endorsed by more than fifty states, built on principles of lawfulness, accountability, safety, reliability, transparency, and the preservation of human judgment. This initiative deliberately parallels the NATO Artificial Intelligence Strategy adopted in 2021, which sets out the allied principles of lawfulness, responsibility, explainability, reliability, governability, and bias mitigation without endorsing prohibition.

Military AI is treated as central to strategic competition with China and Russia. Washington has invested heavily through the Department of Defense Data, Analytics, and AI Adoption Strategy, the Replicator initiative to field thousands of attritable autonomous systems, Combined Joint All-Domain Command and Control (CJADC2), and a legacy of DARPA-led autonomy research dating back decades. The United States views AI-enabled capabilities as essential for deterrence, force protection, intelligence analysis, and maintaining technological superiority in an accelerating global AI arms race.

Within the CCW Group of Governmental Experts (GGE) on LAWS, the United States has been an active participant but a persistent opponent of moving toward treaty negotiations. It favours practical, non-binding measures: clearer discussions of terminology, transparency, testing, and confidence-building. Washington resists broad definitions of LAWS that could capture benign automation and insists that accountability rests with human commanders under existing international law.

## Strategic Interests

- Preserve the technological edge over China and Russia in military AI and autonomy.
- Protect a world-leading defence-industrial base and its artificial intelligence, semiconductor, and software supply chains.
- Prevent a negotiated instrument that restricts legitimate defence innovation or imposes unverifiable commitments.
- Counter proliferation of autonomous capabilities to non-state actors while avoiding constraints on commercial dual-use technology.
- Maintain NATO interoperability and allied consensus on responsible-use principles.
- Prevent miscalculation and unintended escalation between major powers in AI-enabled crises.

## Alliances & Memberships

- **NATO** — co-author of the 2021 AI Strategy; drives responsible-use, interoperability, and information-sharing norms among allies.
- **AUKUS** — technology-sharing pact with the United Kingdom and Australia; increasingly relevant to AI-enabled undersea autonomy.
- **Five Eyes** — intelligence-sharing network coordinating on AI for ISR and targeting analysis.
- **QUAD** — strategic dialogue with India, Japan, and Australia on regional security and technology.
- **Political Declaration on Responsible Military Use of AI and Autonomy** — endorsed by 50+ states as Washington's preferred alternative to a treaty.

## UN Voting Record

The United States has joined consensus on the annual UN General Assembly resolutions on lethal autonomous weapons systems, including A/RES/73/32 and successive texts through A/RES/78/241 (2023) and A/RES/79/62 (2024), supporting the continuation of the CCW Group of Governmental Experts. Its support has consistently been contingent on preserving the GGE mandate and resisting language that would open negotiations on a legally binding instrument. In the GGE, Washington works closely with the United Kingdom, France, and like-minded allies to steer outcomes toward non-binding guiding principles, testing and transparency measures, and endorsement of the political declaration approach. It has declined to support proposals from Austria, Mexico, Brazil, and humanitarian states calling for a prohibitive treaty, arguing that existing international humanitarian law already provides the necessary legal foundation.

## Key Talking Points

- International humanitarian law is technology-neutral and already governs any autonomous weapon; a new treaty is unnecessary.
- Autonomous and AI-assisted systems, developed and used responsibly, can improve accuracy and reduce civilian harm.
- Prohibition would penalize responsible states while irresponsible actors continue development, creating a transparency-free zone.
- Human judgment over the use of force is required and embedded in U.S. doctrine through DoD Directive 3000.09.
- The Political Declaration and responsible-use principles are the most achievable path to international norms.
- The GGE should focus on practical steps — definitional clarity, testing, verification, and confidence-building — not a binding protocol.
- A legally binding treaty would be effectively unverifiable given the dual-use nature of AI.
- The world must guard against an AI arms race, but the answer is responsible competition and deterrence, not unilateral restraint.
