## LAWS & Military AI Stance

Germany is one of the most influential European voices supporting restrictive governance of autonomous weapons. Its position is rooted in a long tradition of international law, multilateral diplomacy, and humanitarian principles, as well as a constitutional commitment to human dignity (Article 1 of the Basic Law) and the historical lessons drawn from the Second World War. Berlin has repeatedly argued that decisions regarding the use of lethal force must remain under meaningful human control and that autonomous systems should not independently make life-and-death decisions (German Federal Foreign Office, 2023).

Germany is a leading advocate of a legally binding instrument on LAWS. Alongside France, it tabled a working paper in 2018 proposing a legally binding protocol to the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW), combining prohibitions on the most dangerous systems with regulations governing acceptable autonomy. Berlin consistently presses the Group of Governmental Experts (GGE) on LAWS to move beyond non-binding guiding principles toward negotiated legal obligations, and it has endorsed international political declarations on the responsible military use of artificial intelligence.

Germany does not advocate eliminating all military autonomy. The Federal Government recognizes the operational advantages of AI-enabled systems, particularly in defensive applications, intelligence analysis, logistics, force protection, and decision support. German policy draws a clear distinction between autonomous assistance, which it supports, and autonomous lethal decision-making, which it seeks to constrain. This balanced approach — restrict the machine's authority over lethal force while preserving military effectiveness — reflects Germany's broader arms control philosophy.

Germany's position is influenced by its role within NATO and the European Union. Following Russia's invasion of Ukraine, Chancellor Scholz announced a historic Zeitenwende (turning point), establishing a €100 billion special fund for the Bundeswehr and committing to the NATO target of spending 2 percent of GDP on defence. The challenge for Berlin is balancing increased military preparedness and technological modernization against its traditional preference for arms control and humanitarian regulation. Germany's national AI strategy (adopted 2018, updated 2020) and its defence innovation efforts seek to develop trustworthy AI capabilities within clear ethical and legal limits.

In international forums, Germany positions itself closer to humanitarian organizations and civil society than most major military powers. It emphasizes that human-machine interaction must be preserved, that accountability for the use of force must remain with persons, and that unpredictability in weapons systems violates international humanitarian law. Germany supports the EU's "trustworthy AI" framing, which stresses human agency, robustness, transparency, and accountability, and it applies these principles to defence applications.

## Strategic Interests

- Preserving Germany's identity as a civilian, rule-of-law power committed to arms control, disarmament, and humanitarian protection.
- Ensuring that international regulation does not leave democratic states technologically disadvantaged against potential adversaries.
- Maintaining NATO cohesion and interoperability while the alliance adopts AI for decision support, intelligence, and logistics.
- Preventing an unregulated arms race in military AI and autonomous weapons, especially in light of Russia's invasion of Ukraine.
- Protecting the Bundeswehr's ability to modernize (€100 billion special fund, 2 percent NATO spending target) within clear legal and ethical bounds.
- Reinforcing the primacy of international humanitarian law and the principle of human responsibility in the use of force.

## Alliances & Memberships

- **NATO** — Germany works within the alliance's 2021 Artificial Intelligence Strategy, which stresses lawfulness, responsibility, explainability, and human accountability rather than prohibition.
- **European Union** — Applies the EU's "trustworthy AI" framework and human-oversight requirements to security and defence technologies; coordinates positions in the First Committee with EU partners.
- **CCW / GGE on LAWS** — Core negotiating venue; Germany pushes for a mandate to negotiate a legally binding instrument.
- **OSCE** — Supports confidence-building measures on emerging military technologies in Europe.
- **G7** — Uses G7 frameworks, including the Hiroshima AI Process, to advance responsible AI governance globally.

## UN Voting Record

Germany has consistently voted in favour of and co-sponsored the annual General Assembly resolutions on lethal autonomous weapons systems, including A/RES/78/241 (2023) and A/RES/79/62 (2024), which call for urgent negotiations within the CCW framework. In the First Committee, Germany typically aligns with the European Union bloc, supporting language on human control, international humanitarian law, and the negotiation of legally binding measures. Berlin has repeatedly called for the GGE to be upgraded and mandated to negotiate, warning that voluntary principles alone are insufficient given the speed of technological development.

## Key Talking Points

- Machines must never independently decide to take a human life; meaningful human control is non-negotiable.
- The GGE should be mandated to negotiate a legally binding instrument combining prohibitions and regulations under the CCW.
- Voluntary political declarations are welcome but cannot substitute for binding international law.
- AI-enabled defence capabilities offer real benefits in intelligence, logistics, and force protection and should be developed responsibly.
- Unpredictable or uncontrolled autonomous systems raise grave risks of accidental escalation, proliferation, and accountability gaps.
- International humanitarian law — distinction, proportionality, and precaution — must apply to all weapons systems, including AI-enabled ones.
- European and transatlantic coordination on military AI governance prevents fragmentation and preserves democratic technological advantage.
