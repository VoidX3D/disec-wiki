## LAWS & Military AI Stance

Kenya engages the LAWS debate from the dual perspective of an emerging African technology hub and a state confronting real security threats. Nairobi has built a reputation as the "Silicon Savannah", with a dynamic technology sector, a world-leading mobile-money ecosystem, and growing capabilities in cybersecurity and digital governance. At the same time, Kenya faces terrorism from al-Shabaab along its border with Somalia, banditry, and regional instability, and it is a major contributor to regional peacekeeping operations, including in Somalia.

Kenya has developed technological capabilities in cybersecurity and surveillance and has been an active participant in UN and continental discussions on cybersecurity and digital governance, including the UN Open-ended Working Group on ICT security. This experience leads Nairobi to approach military AI with an emphasis on oversight, international cooperation, and the prevention of misuse, rather than on autonomous weapons development, which is not a Kenyan priority.

Kenya supports meaningful human control and international oversight of autonomous weapons, aligning with the African Group's humanitarian and governance-oriented position. Nairobi argues that autonomous weapons could increase the vulnerability of weaker states, intensify conflict by lowering the political and human costs of using force, and create dangerous new opportunities for non-state actors. It calls for transparency, accountability, and inclusive governance in which African and developing states have a genuine voice.

Given its role as a peacekeeping contributor, Kenya is particularly concerned about the protection of peacekeepers and civilians in conflict zones, and it warns that autonomous systems used by adversaries could endanger uniformed personnel deployed under UN and regional mandates. This operational experience lends credibility to Kenya's calls for strong safeguards and human control.

Kenya balances its humanitarian concerns with recognition that AI has legitimate uses for development and security, including cybersecurity and border management. Its position is therefore measured: it supports a legally binding outcome that preserves human control and protects developing states, while insisting that technology transfer and capacity-building accompany any regulatory regime.

## Strategic Interests

- Preventing proliferation of autonomous weapons and drones to terrorist and non-state actors in the region.
- Protecting Kenyan and regional peacekeeping personnel from autonomous threats.
- Strengthening cybersecurity governance as part of the military AI agenda.
- Ensuring African states are represented in global AI and ICT governance.
- Preventing a technological divide that increases the vulnerability of developing states.
- Supporting inclusive, capacity-building-oriented regulation.

## Alliances & Memberships

- **African Union / IGAD / EAC:** Regional coordination on security, counterterrorism, and disarmament positions.
- **UN Open-ended Working Group on ICT security:** Active participation on cyber and emerging-technology governance.
- **CCW GGE:** Participant supporting oversight-oriented regulation and human control.
- **Commonwealth / G77+China:** Broader forums for coordinating Global South positions.
- **UN peacekeeping (major troop contributor):** Operational stake in protecting personnel from autonomous threats.

## UN Voting Record

Kenya voted in favour of A/RES/78/241 (2023) and A/RES/79/62 (2024) on lethal autonomous weapons systems and supports the General Assembly's consultative process and meetings of States. In the GGE and First Committee, Nairobi backs language on meaningful human control, accountability, and transparency, and it supports a legally binding outcome consistent with the African Group position. Kenya generally votes with the humanitarian-leaning Global South majority, and it draws on its cybersecurity diplomacy to urge that military AI governance and cyber governance be treated as linked challenges.

## Key Talking Points

- As the Silicon Savannah, Kenya knows the power of technology — and the danger of it being misused.
- Autonomous weapons must not proliferate to terrorist groups like al-Shabaab.
- Kenyan peacekeepers must never face autonomous weapons without the protection of international law.
- Meaningful human control and accountability are essential and must be legally binding.
- Cybersecurity and military AI governance are two sides of the same problem.
- African states need capacity-building and technology transfer, not just prohibitions.
- A new technological divide would make weaker states more vulnerable to autonomous warfare.
- Kenya supports inclusive, transparent global governance for AI and autonomy.
