## LAWS & Military AI Stance

Pakistan approaches military AI through the perspective of regional security competition, particularly its strategic relationship with India. As a nuclear-armed state, Islamabad considers technological modernization essential for maintaining the military balance in South Asia.

Pakistan has explored AI applications through its defence research institutions, focusing on autonomous systems, surveillance technologies, cybersecurity, and unmanned aerial vehicles. The country has developed considerable experience with drone technology and recognizes the growing importance of AI-enabled warfare for offsetting conventional asymmetries.

Pakistan generally supports international discussions on regulating autonomous weapons but does not support restrictions that could disadvantage developing states. Islamabad emphasizes equal access to technology, responsible use, and the prevention of monopolization of advanced military capabilities by major powers.

Pakistan's position reflects broader Global South concerns that arms control frameworks should not create technological inequality between advanced and developing states. It is particularly sensitive to any instrument that would freeze existing asymmetries in military AI between Pakistan and its principal rival.

As a nuclear-armed non-NPT party that is not a State Party to the CCW, Pakistan engages the LAWS debate primarily through the General Assembly. It favours politically binding principles, confidence-building measures, and capacity-building provisions over a rushed legally binding instrument that could cement major-power advantage.

## Strategic Interests

- Strategic parity and deterrence vis-à-vis India in both conventional and AI-enabled domains.
- Equal access to AI and dual-use technologies for developing states.
- Preventing major powers from monopolizing military AI capabilities.
- Sustaining UAV and drone capability development for surveillance and asymmetric defence.
- Leadership among Global South and NAM states on technology equity in arms control.
- Preserving nuclear deterrence and strategic options outside the NPT framework.
- Counter-terrorism and stability across South Asia and Afghanistan.

## Alliances & Memberships

- Non-Aligned Movement (NAM): the principal frame for Pakistan's Global South positions on technology access and equity.
- Organisation of Islamic Cooperation (OIC): solidarity with Muslim-majority states on security issues.
- Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO): regional security dialogue, including with China and Central Asian states.
- Not a CCW Party: Pakistan therefore engages LAWS mainly through UNGA and the First Committee rather than the CCW GGE.

## UN Voting Record

Pakistan has generally voted in favour of the consensus resolutions on lethal autonomous weapons systems in the First Committee, including A/RES/78/241 (2023) and A/RES/79/62 (2024). Islamabad supports the thrust of these resolutions — reaffirming human responsibility and accountability — but consistently resists provisions that would mandate immediate negotiation of a legally binding instrument.

Pakistan's diplomacy emphasizes capacity building, technology transfer, and equal access, warning that premature or overly rigid regulation could entrench the technological dominance of a few powers. As a non-CCW party, it presses for inclusive, transparent processes in which states outside the convention can meaningfully participate.

## Key Talking Points

- "Regulation must not create a two-tier world in which a few powers dominate military AI."
- "Pakistan supports responsible use, equal access, and technology transfer for developing states."
- "A rushed binding treaty could cement existing technological inequalities."
- "Autonomous and drone technologies are legitimate means of self-defence in an unequal strategic environment."
- "Human control and accountability are essential, but so is fairness in access."
- "The CCW process must be inclusive and transparent to all states, including non-parties."
- "Confidence-building and transparency matter more than premature prohibition."
- "Any framework must respect the right of developing states to modernize their defence capabilities."
