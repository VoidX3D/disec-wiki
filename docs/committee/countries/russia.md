## LAWS & Military AI Stance

Russia approaches LAWS and military AI regulation primarily through the lens of strategic stability and national security, and it has generally opposed legally binding restrictions. Moscow argues that existing international humanitarian law is sufficient to regulate emerging military technologies and that additional restrictions could limit legitimate defence capabilities. This position reflects a determination to preserve maximum freedom of action in military modernization and to prevent negotiations from being used to freeze imbalances between great powers.

At the national level, Russia has invested steadily in autonomous vehicles, robotics, unmanned systems, electronic warfare, and AI-enabled military platforms. Programmes such as the Marker robotic combat platform, the extensive fielding of strike and reconnaissance drones, and advanced electronic warfare systems demonstrate Moscow's view of AI as a critical component of future warfare. Russian military thinking treats information superiority, algorithmic targeting, and automation as essential to competing with NATO, which Moscow perceives as enjoying technological advantages.

Russia's experience in the war in Ukraine has accelerated this trajectory. The conflict has seen extensive Russian use of drones, electronic warfare, automated surveillance, and AI-assisted intelligence analysis. Moscow uses this experience both to justify continued development and to argue that all major powers, including Western states, are integrating autonomy into warfare, which makes prohibition unrealistic and self-defeating for those who would comply.

Within the CCW Group of Governmental Experts (GGE) on LAWS, Russia participates but remains cautious. It supports non-binding principles, transparency discussions, and confidence-building measures, while resisting mandates that would lead to treaty negotiations. Moscow rejects political declarations it views as attempting to establish binding obligations without its consent, including the U.S.-led Political Declaration on Responsible Military Use of AI and Autonomy, which Russia has not endorsed.

Russia emphasizes sovereignty and strategic independence in every forum. It argues that any international regulation must respect each state's right to determine its own defence programmes and that the dual-use nature of AI makes verification effectively impossible. While using humanitarian language in general terms, Moscow consistently warns against restricting autonomous capabilities that serve legitimate national defence.

## Strategic Interests

- Preserve unrestricted freedom of action in AI, robotics, drones, and electronic warfare development.
- Prevent international rules from consolidating U.S. and Western technological dominance.
- Counter NATO's perceived AI and autonomy advantage through continued modernization.
- Use battlefield experience from Ukraine to shape arguments that prohibition is unrealistic.
- Maintain the CCW GGE as a consensus-based forum that does not impose binding outcomes.
- Prevent autonomous-capability proliferation to non-state actors without accepting restrictive commitments.

## Alliances & Memberships

- **Collective Security Treaty Organization (CSTO)** — security bloc with Central Asian partners for common positions on emerging threats.
- **Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO)** — coordination with China and Central Asian states on sovereignty-based approaches.
- **BRICS** — platform for non-Western alignment on technology governance and resistance to Western norm-setting.
- **Union State with Belarus** — integrated defence cooperation, including shared military technology development.

## UN Voting Record

Russia has joined consensus on the annual UN General Assembly resolutions on lethal autonomous weapons systems, including A/RES/78/241 (2023) and A/RES/79/62 (2024), supporting the continuation of the GGE process. Its support, however, has been coupled with consistent resistance inside the GGE to any mandate to negotiate a legally binding instrument. Moscow favours politically non-binding principles, the reaffirmation of the applicability of international humanitarian law, and confidence-building measures. It has declined to endorse Western-sponsored instruments such as the Political Declaration on Responsible Military Use of AI and Autonomy, and it generally opposes language in resolutions that implies an obligation to negotiate new law for autonomous weapons.

## Key Talking Points

- International humanitarian law already governs all weapons systems, including autonomous ones; no new treaty is needed.
- Regulation must not become a tool for great powers to freeze the existing balance of military technology.
- Without a universally accepted definition of LAWS, a binding instrument would be premature and unverifiable.
- The dual-use nature of AI makes effective verification and enforcement impossible.
- All major military powers are integrating autonomy into warfare, so unilateral prohibition is unrealistic.
- Politically binding declarations that create obligations without consent must be rejected.
- Support confidence-building measures, transparency, and state responsibility under international law.
- An AI arms race must be prevented through mutual restraint, not through one-sided restrictions on legitimate defence.
