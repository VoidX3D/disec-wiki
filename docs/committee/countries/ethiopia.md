## LAWS & Military AI Stance

Ethiopia approaches military AI and autonomous weapons primarily from the perspective of peace, development, and regional stability. As a founding member of the UN, host of the African Union headquarters in Addis Ababa, and a state that has faced serious internal conflict — including the 2020–2022 war in the Tigray region — Ethiopia is acutely aware of the human costs of modern warfare and the dangers of destabilising military technology. It is not a significant developer of autonomous weapons and does not prioritise their development.

Addis Ababa aligns closely with the African Union's emphasis on responsible technology governance and on ensuring that emerging technologies contribute to development rather than instability. Ethiopia argues that autonomous weapons could increase the vulnerability of weaker states, lower the threshold for the use of force, and intensify conflicts by reducing the human and political costs of war. It supports international oversight and meaningful human control over lethal decisions.

Ethiopia's security challenges — including border disputes, terrorism linked to al-Shabaab, and internal instability in the wider Horn of Africa — shape its interest in preventing the proliferation of autonomous systems to non-state actors and to hostile regional forces. It welcomes international rules that protect states with limited technological capacity from being destabilised by inexpensive autonomous weapons.

While Ethiopia has shown interest in defence modernisation and technological development, its priorities remain national security, economic development, and regional stability. Addis Ababa generally supports consensus-based regulation in the CCW GGE and the First Committee, backing humanitarian-leaning language on accountability and transparency while avoiding ambitious commitments that would burden a developing state with limited capacity.

As host of the African Union, Ethiopia also sees itself as a guardian of African institutional diplomacy and works to ensure that continental positions on AI and disarmament are advanced in UN settings, consistent with the African Union's framework for responsible technology governance.

## Strategic Interests

- Preventing destabilisation of the Horn of Africa by the proliferation of autonomous and drone systems.
- Avoiding an arms race in autonomous weapons that would burden developing states.
- Ensuring technology serves development, not instability, per the African Union framework.
- Maintaining human control and accountability over lethal force.
- Protecting Ethiopian and African sovereignty in the face of technological inequality.
- Supporting peace and security after its own recent internal conflict.

## Alliances & Memberships

- **African Union (host state):** Central role in shaping and advancing continental positions on technology governance.
- **IGAD:** Regional security cooperation on the Horn of Africa, including counterterrorism and border security.
- **Non-Aligned Movement / G77+China:** Solidarity with Global South disarmament positions.
- **CCW GGE:** Participant supporting oversight-oriented, consensus-based regulation.
- **United Nations First Committee:** Active African voice in disarmament debates.

## UN Voting Record

Ethiopia voted in favour of A/RES/78/241 (2023) and A/RES/79/62 (2024) on lethal autonomous weapons systems and supports the General Assembly's consultative process and meetings of States. In the GGE and First Committee, Addis Ababa backs consensus principles on human responsibility, IHL applicability, and accountability, and it supports a legally binding outcome aligned with the African Group position. Ethiopia generally votes with the humanitarian-leaning Global South majority on LAWS resolutions.

## Key Talking Points

- The Horn of Africa cannot become a proving ground for autonomous weapons.
- Autonomous systems that lower the threshold for using force will intensify Africa's conflicts, not resolve them.
- Ethiopia, as AU host, calls for a strong African position on responsible technology governance.
- Meaningful human control and accountability are non-negotiable for lethal systems.
- Inexpensive autonomous weapons must not proliferate to non-state actors and terrorist groups.
- A new technological divide would leave developing states like Ethiopia more vulnerable, not safer.
- Regulation should be realistic for states with limited capacity — consensus, transparency, and support.
- Ethiopia supports a legally binding instrument in line with African and Global South consensus.
