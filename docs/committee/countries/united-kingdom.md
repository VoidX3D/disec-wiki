## LAWS & Military AI Stance

The United Kingdom was the first state to publish a formal military doctrine on autonomous systems, issuing Joint Concept Note 1/18, "Human-Machine Teaming," in 2018. This document established the UK position that humans should remain accountable for the use of force and that AI and autonomy should be used to augment, rather than replace, human decision-making. The doctrine deliberately rejected both a blanket ban on autonomous weapons and the idea that machines could be held legally responsible, anchoring accountability in human commanders and operators.

London supports a regulatory framework based on responsible use, transparency, rigorous testing, and human oversight, and it opposes a legally binding prohibition. The UK argues that international humanitarian law is technology-neutral and already applies to all weapons systems, including autonomous ones, and that the priority should be ensuring systems can comply with the principles of distinction, proportionality, and precaution rather than negotiating a new treaty.

The UK has an active national military AI programme, centred on the Defence AI Centre established in 2022 and the wider Defence AI Strategy. Applications include autonomous drones and "loyal wingman" concepts for the future combat air system, AI-assisted intelligence analysis, predictive logistics, and cyber defence. London views AI-enabled capabilities as essential force multipliers and as critical for maintaining interoperability with NATO allies and partners.

In the CCW Group of Governmental Experts (GGE) on LAWS, the United Kingdom has been a constructive and pragmatic participant. It supports continued discussion of definitions, transparency, and confidence-building measures, and it has endorsed the U.S. Political Declaration on Responsible Military Use of AI and Autonomy. The UK generally aligns with the United States and France in favouring non-binding, responsible-use outcomes rather than treaty negotiations, while remaining open to incremental normative progress.

British policy emphasizes human-machine teaming as the governing concept: autonomy should be pursued where it improves safety, effectiveness, and the protection of military personnel, while humans must retain control over lethal decision-making. London also stresses the importance of legal review of weapons systems under Article 36 of Additional Protocol I and of rigorous testing to ensure autonomous systems behave predictably.

## Strategic Interests

- Preserve freedom to develop and export AI-enabled defence technology, including autonomous air and maritime systems.
- Maintain interoperability with NATO and Five Eyes partners in AI-enabled operations.
- Leverage AUKUS technology-sharing with the United States and Australia for undersea autonomy and AI.
- Prevent a legally binding instrument from constraining legitimate defence innovation.
- Shape international norms toward responsible use, testing, and transparency rather than prohibition.
- Counter proliferation of autonomous weapons to non-state actors through export controls and standards.

## Alliances & Memberships

- **NATO** — active contributor to the 2021 AI Strategy; relies on allied AI interoperability and shared responsible-use principles.
- **AUKUS** — trilateral technology-sharing pact with the United States and Australia relevant to autonomous undersea systems and AI.
- **Five Eyes** — intelligence alliance coordinating on AI-enabled ISR and targeting analysis.
- **Commonwealth** — network for defence cooperation and capacity-building with partner states.
- **Political Declaration on Responsible Military Use of AI and Autonomy** — endorsed, supporting the U.S.-led responsible-use approach.

## UN Voting Record

The United Kingdom has voted in favour of and joined consensus on the annual UN General Assembly resolutions on lethal autonomous weapons systems, including A/RES/78/241 (2023) and A/RES/79/62 (2024), supporting the continuation of the CCW Group of Governmental Experts. In the GGE, the UK has aligned with the United States and France in resisting negotiations on a legally binding instrument, arguing that existing international humanitarian law applies and that the process should focus on practical, non-binding measures such as transparency, testing, and confidence-building. London has endorsed the Political Declaration on Responsible Military Use of AI and Autonomy as the preferred vehicle for international norm-building.

## Key Talking Points

- Human-machine teaming is the future of defence; humans must remain accountable for lethal decision-making.
- International humanitarian law is technology-neutral and already applies to autonomous weapons.
- A blanket prohibition would harm legitimate defence innovation and allied interoperability.
- The UK was the first state to publish doctrine on military autonomy — a model for responsible use.
- Support transparency, rigorous testing, and legal review of weapons systems as practical priorities.
- Definitional progress should be incremental, not driven by a premature treaty.
- Backed the Political Declaration on Responsible Military Use of AI and Autonomy as effective norm-building.
- Autonomous systems must be designed, tested, and deployed so they can comply with distinction and proportionality.
