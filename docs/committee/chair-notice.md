# Chair Notice

Message from the Chair

## United Nations General Assembly – First Committee (DISEC)

Agenda: Regulating Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS) and Military Artificial Intelligence

Dear Esteemed Delegates,

It is with immense pride and great enthusiasm that I welcome each one of you to the Disarmament and International Security Committee (DISEC). As your Chair, it is both an honour and a privilege to embark on this intellectual and diplomatic journey alongside delegates representing diverse nations, perspectives, and strategic interests. I congratulate each of you for taking the initiative to participate in a committee that lies at the heart of contemporary international peace and security.

The agenda before us: Regulating Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS) and Military Artificial Intelligence is not merely another discussion on emerging technologies. It represents one of the defining security challenges of the twenty-first century. Never before has humanity possessed technologies capable of transforming warfare at such unprecedented speed while simultaneously challenging the very foundations upon which international humanitarian law, ethical responsibility, military accountability, and global governance have been constructed. Artificial intelligence has already begun reshaping civilian life through innovation and economic advancement; however, its rapid integration into military capabilities introduces profound legal, ethical, strategic, humanitarian, and geopolitical questions that the international community can no longer afford to postpone.

Throughout history, technological revolutions have consistently altered the character of warfare. From gunpowder to mechanized warfare, from nuclear weapons to cyber operations, every transformative innovation has compelled states to reconsider existing legal frameworks and strategic doctrines. Today, autonomous military technologies represent the latest evolution in this continuum. Unlike previous military innovations, however, Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems possess the unprecedented ability to identify, select, and potentially engage targets with varying degrees of human involvement. This development forces the international community to confront an essential question: Should decisions over human life and death ever be delegated to machines?

This committee is not expected to decide whether artificial intelligence itself is beneficial or harmful. Military AI offers remarkable opportunities for improved logistics, intelligence analysis, cyber defence, surveillance, humanitarian assistance, disaster response, and force protection. At the same time, the deployment of AI-enabled autonomous weapons raises significant concerns regarding accountability, civilian protection, algorithmic reliability, escalation risks, strategic stability, and compliance with International Humanitarian Law (IHL). Your responsibility as delegates is therefore not to reject technological progress, but to determine how the international community can responsibly govern it.

The Study Guide prepared for this committee has been designed as a comprehensive academic and diplomatic resource consisting of 95 pages. It is intentionally detailed because the complexity of this agenda demands far more than superficial understanding.

Rather than simply presenting facts, the guide aims to provide delegates with the historical evolution, geopolitical perspectives, legal foundations, military developments, ethical debates, institutional efforts, and future policy options necessary to engage in sophisticated and evidence-based diplomacy.

While I strongly encourage every delegate to study the entire guide, I would also like to emphasize several sections that deserve particular attention, as they will serve as the foundation of meaningful committee discussions.

You should first develop a thorough understanding of the historical evolution of military artificial intelligence and autonomous weapons presented in Part II. Understanding how military automation evolved from early automated defence systems to today's AI-enabled autonomous platforms provides the necessary context for appreciating why regulation has become an urgent international priority. Pay particular attention to the progression of military drones, machine learning applications, AI swarm technologies, varying levels of human control, and the broader role of AI beyond autonomous weapon systems. These developments explain why technological capability has advanced faster than international governance mechanisms.

Equally important is Part III, which examines the positions of major powers, regional organizations, and geopolitical blocs. This committee will not succeed if delegates approach the agenda solely from humanitarian or technological perspectives. Every country's policy is shaped by its security environment, military doctrine, economic capabilities, alliance commitments, regional rivalries, and strategic priorities. The positions adopted by the United States, China, Russia, the United Kingdom, France, NATO members, Indo-Pacific states, Middle Eastern countries, African nations, Latin American governments, and the broader Global South often differ substantially not because they disagree on the importance of international security, but because they perceive risks and strategic interests differently. Understanding these distinctions is essential for realistic diplomacy and effective coalition-building.

Another section deserving your careful attention is Part VI, which explores the existing international legal and institutional framework governing autonomous weapons and military AI. Delegates should become familiar with the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW), the Group of Governmental Experts (GGE), International Humanitarian Law, the Geneva Conventions, the Arms Trade Treaty, UNIDIR, the International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC), OECD AI Principles, UNESCO's AI Ethics Framework, and NATO's AI Strategy. A successful resolution cannot exist in isolation; it must complement, strengthen, or build upon existing international legal instruments rather than duplicate or contradict them.

Perhaps the most critical analytical component of the Study Guide is Part VII, which presents the core challenges associated with Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems. Delegates should devote particular attention to the accountability gap, meaningful human control, civilian protection, algorithmic bias, cybersecurity vulnerabilities, proliferation risks, verification difficulties, strategic instability, and the absence of universally accepted definitions. These issues are likely to become central themes throughout formal debate, moderated caucuses, and draft resolution negotiations. Strong delegates will not merely identify these problems but will explain how they interact and why solving one challenge often affects several others simultaneously.

Closely connected to these concerns is Part VIII, which examines contemporary global developments. Modern conflicts including the Ukraine–Russia conflict, AI-assisted military operations in the Middle East, strategic competition between the United States and China, evolving China–Taiwan tensions, autonomous drone warfare, AI-enabled cyber operations, and the increasing technological capabilities of non-state actors illustrate that this agenda is no longer theoretical. Military AI is already influencing strategic decision-making, battlefield operations, and international diplomacy. Delegates should therefore avoid treating autonomous weapons as a distant future concern; instead, recognize them as an evolving reality that already shapes contemporary security.

The committee's discussion should ultimately be guided by the questions presented in Parts IX, X, and XI, which collectively examine the consequences of military AI, the urgency of international action, and possible pathways toward effective governance. As you prepare your country's position, I encourage you to think beyond simple calls for bans or unrestricted technological development. Consider whether international regulation should distinguish between offensive and defensive autonomous systems. Reflect on what constitutes meaningful human control and how it could be operationally defined. Examine whether legally binding treaties, political declarations, technical standards, confidence-building measures, export controls, transparency mechanisms, international verification systems, ethical principles, or combinations of these approaches offer the most practical path forward.

When drafting resolutions, delegates should strive for solutions that are realistic, implementable, and internationally acceptable. Strong resolutions acknowledge geopolitical realities while advancing humanitarian objectives. They balance technological innovation with responsible governance. They protect civilian populations without unnecessarily restricting legitimate national security requirements. They recognize the different capacities of technologically advanced and developing states while promoting equitable participation in future governance mechanisms.

I also encourage every delegate to make full use of the Country Matrix included in the Study Guide. This section has been specifically prepared to assist delegates in understanding their assigned country's strategic priorities, military capabilities, diplomatic positions, regional partnerships, and likely negotiating preferences. Remaining faithful to national policy while demonstrating diplomatic flexibility is one of the defining characteristics of successful Model United Nations participation.

Throughout this conference, I hope you remember that diplomacy is not measured solely by the number of speeches delivered or amendments proposed. The most impactful delegates are those who listen carefully, negotiate respectfully, analyse critically, and seek common ground even amid profound disagreement. The purpose of DISEC is not simply to debate conflict but to reduce the risks that threaten international peace and security through constructive multilateral cooperation.

As representatives of the international community, you have been entrusted with addressing one of the most consequential technological and security challenges of our generation. The discussions you engage in over the coming sessions should reflect intellectual integrity, diplomatic professionalism, and a genuine commitment to building a safer international order. Challenge assumptions, question existing frameworks, support your arguments with evidence, respect differing viewpoints, and never lose sight of the human consequences that lie behind every policy decision.

I have every confidence that this committee will produce debates characterized by depth, diplomacy, and innovation. I encourage each of you to approach this agenda with curiosity, determination, and an unwavering commitment to excellence. Let this committee become a platform where strategic interests meet humanitarian responsibility, where technological advancement is balanced by ethical governance, and where thoughtful diplomacy contributes to shaping the future of international security.

On behalf of the Executive Board, I wish you the very best in your preparation. I eagerly look forward to witnessing informed debate, meaningful collaboration, and innovative resolutions from each of you.

I wish you all the very best, and I look forward to welcoming you to committee.

Warm regards,

Chairperson

Disarmament and International Security Committee (DISEC)
