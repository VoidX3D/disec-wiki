# Part III-D: Middle East, Africa, Latin America, and Global South

The perspectives of the Middle East, Africa, Latin America, and the wider Global South on regulating Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS) and Military Artificial Intelligence differ significantly from those of technologically advanced military powers. While many states in these regions recognize the transformative potential of artificial intelligence and autonomous technologies, their approaches are often shaped by concerns regarding sovereignty, technological inequality, historical experiences with foreign intervention, regional instability, humanitarian consequences of warfare, and the unequal distribution of advanced military capabilities. Unlike major powers that primarily view military AI through the lens of strategic competition and defence modernization, many developing countries emphasize preventing destabilization, ensuring equitable access to technology, strengthening international law, and avoiding a future in which warfare becomes dominated by states possessing superior computational and technological resources.

The Global South has generally demonstrated greater openness toward stronger international regulation of autonomous weapons. Many developing states argue that the rapid militarization of artificial intelligence risks creating a new technological divide in which powerful countries acquire advanced autonomous capabilities while weaker states become increasingly vulnerable. Consequently, several countries from Africa, Latin America, and parts of Asia have supported discussions emphasizing meaningful human control, accountability, transparency, and the development of international legal frameworks governing autonomous weapons.

However, positions within the Global South are not completely uniform. Some emerging powers, such as India, Brazil, and the United Arab Emirates, are actively developing technological capabilities and therefore seek regulatory frameworks that do not prevent domestic innovation. Others, particularly smaller developing states, prioritize humanitarian concerns and advocate stronger restrictions. These differences demonstrate that the debate over military AI is not simply divided between developed and developing countries but reflects diverse strategic interests.

Israel

Israel represents one of the most technologically advanced military powers in the Middle East and has become a significant actor in the development of autonomous and AI-enabled defence technologies. The country’s security environment, characterized by persistent regional tensions, asymmetric conflicts, and advanced missile threats, has encouraged substantial investment in artificial intelligence, unmanned systems, surveillance technologies, and autonomous platforms.

Israel has developed advanced capabilities in areas such as drone technology, border surveillance, missile defence, intelligence analysis, and AI-assisted targeting systems. Israeli defence companies are among the world leaders in autonomous aerial systems, robotics, and military electronics. These capabilities have made Israel an important case study in discussions regarding the operational use of AI in modern warfare.

Israel generally opposes legally binding international restrictions that could limit military innovation. It argues that autonomous technologies can provide significant defensive advantages and may reduce risks to military personnel and civilians when used responsibly. Israeli policymakers emphasize that existing international humanitarian law already provides sufficient standards for regulating weapons systems and that the focus should remain on responsible deployment rather than prohibition.

However, Israel also recognizes the importance of human oversight and accountability. Its position is generally consistent with technologically advanced states that support human involvement while rejecting restrictions that could constrain defence capabilities. Israel argues that autonomous systems may improve precision, reduce collateral damage, and enhance protection of civilian populations through faster and more accurate threat identification.

The Israeli position reflects the broader challenge facing states operating in high-threat environments: while humanitarian concerns are acknowledged, national security priorities create strong incentives to continue developing advanced military technologies.

Iran

Iran’s approach toward military AI is shaped by regional competition, strategic deterrence, and efforts to develop asymmetric military capabilities despite economic sanctions and technological limitations. Although Iran does not possess the same technological resources as the United States, China, or Israel, it has invested significantly in unmanned aerial vehicles, missile technology, cyber capabilities, and indigenous defence innovation.

Iran has demonstrated extensive use of drone technologies and has developed capabilities that combine autonomous navigation, surveillance, and precision strike functions. These developments have increased international attention regarding the proliferation of relatively inexpensive autonomous systems among regional powers.

Iran generally supports international discussions on regulating autonomous weapons but remains cautious regarding restrictions that could limit technological development. Tehran frequently emphasizes principles of sovereignty, equality among states, and opposition to technological monopolization by advanced powers.

From Iran’s perspective, strict limitations imposed by technologically dominant states could reinforce existing inequalities in military capability. Therefore, Iran supports regulations that prevent misuse of autonomous weapons but resists frameworks that could restrict access to advanced defence technologies.

Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates

The Gulf region has become increasingly important in discussions regarding military AI due to significant defence spending, strategic competition, and investment in emerging technologies. Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates have pursued ambitious programmes aimed at developing advanced technological capabilities, including artificial intelligence, autonomous systems, cybersecurity, and defence innovation.

Saudi Arabia views AI as an important component of its broader economic modernization strategy, including the development of domestic technological industries under Vision 2030. The country has invested in AI research, digital transformation, and defence modernization. Saudi Arabia supports responsible AI development but generally does not favour restrictions that could limit technological advancement.

The United Arab Emirates has emerged as one of the most technologically ambitious states in the Middle East. The UAE has invested heavily in artificial intelligence research, autonomous systems, robotics, and defence technology. It has established itself as a regional leader in AI development and has explored military applications including autonomous vehicles, surveillance systems, and unmanned platforms.

Both Saudi Arabia and the UAE support international cooperation but emphasize maintaining strategic flexibility. Their positions reflect the interests of emerging technological powers seeking to develop indigenous capabilities while participating in global governance discussions.

Egypt

Egypt represents an important regional actor in North Africa and the Middle East due to its strategic location, military influence, and role in regional security. Egypt has shown increasing interest in defence modernization, including unmanned systems, surveillance technologies, and digital security.

Egypt’s position generally emphasizes sovereignty, national defence priorities, and regional stability. While Cairo supports international discussions on emerging military technologies, it remains cautious regarding restrictions that could affect defence modernization.

Egypt’s approach aligns with many developing states that support regulation but oppose frameworks perceived as creating unequal technological advantages for advanced military powers.

Africa: Regional Perspectives

African countries generally approach military AI and autonomous weapons from the perspective of peace, development, and preventing new forms of technological inequality. Unlike major powers engaged in strategic competition, most African states possess limited military AI capabilities and prioritize economic development, cybersecurity, and humanitarian concerns.

The African Union has emphasized responsible technology governance and the importance of ensuring that emerging technologies contribute to development rather than instability. Many African states are concerned that autonomous weapons could increase the vulnerability of weaker states and intensify conflicts by reducing the political and human costs associated with the use of force.

South Africa

South Africa has traditionally played an important role in international arms control and disarmament diplomacy. Its historical experience with nuclear disarmament and commitment to multilateral governance influence its approach toward autonomous weapons.

South Africa has generally supported stronger international regulation of LAWS and emphasizes the importance of maintaining human control over lethal decisions. Pretoria argues that autonomous weapons raise serious ethical and humanitarian concerns, and that international law must evolve to address emerging technologies.

As one of Africa’s leading diplomatic actors, South Africa frequently represents broader Global South concerns regarding accountability, transparency, and preventing technological domination by powerful states.

Nigeria

Nigeria’s perspective is influenced by counterterrorism challenges, internal security concerns, and the need to manage emerging technologies responsibly. As Africa’s largest economy and a major regional security actor, Nigeria recognizes the importance of advanced technologies in addressing security threats.

Nigeria has utilized surveillance technologies and unmanned systems for security purposes but remains cautious regarding fully autonomous lethal systems. Its priorities include preventing proliferation to non-state actors, strengthening cybersecurity, and ensuring that technological development supports peace and stability.

Nigeria’s position reflects many developing states that recognize the usefulness of AI while emphasizing international oversight.

Ethiopia and Kenya

Ethiopia and Kenya represent East African perspectives on military technology governance. Both countries face security challenges involving terrorism, border security, and regional instability.

Kenya has developed technological capabilities in cybersecurity and surveillance while participating in regional peace and security initiatives. Ethiopia has also demonstrated interest in defence modernization and technological development.

However, neither country is likely to prioritize autonomous weapons development. Their focus remains on national security, development, and maintaining regional stability.

Latin America

Latin American countries have historically maintained strong commitments to international law, peaceful dispute resolution, and arms control. The region is generally less militarized compared to other regions and has limited involvement in developing autonomous weapons.

Countries such as Brazil, Argentina, and Mexico emphasize preventing destabilizing arms races and ensuring that emerging technologies remain subject to international governance.

Brazil

Brazil represents the largest defence actor in Latin America and has growing technological capabilities. Brazil has invested in artificial intelligence, aerospace technology, cybersecurity, and defence innovation.

Brazil supports international regulation of autonomous weapons but generally avoids supporting complete prohibition. It emphasizes that developing countries should not be excluded from technological progress and that regulation should prevent misuse rather than restrict innovation.

Brazil’s diplomatic tradition emphasizes multilateralism, strategic autonomy, and equitable international governance.

Argentina and Mexico

Argentina has historically supported arms control and peaceful international relations. Its approach toward autonomous weapons emphasizes humanitarian considerations, transparency, and international cooperation.

Mexico has generally adopted a humanitarian approach toward emerging weapons technologies. It has supported stronger restrictions on autonomous weapons and emphasizes the importance of preventing machines from independently deciding to use lethal force.

Mexico’s position aligns closely with countries advocating greater international legal regulation.

Global South Collective Perspective

The broader Global South position reflects several common themes. First, many developing states are concerned about technological inequality. They argue that advanced military AI capabilities should not become instruments reinforcing existing global power imbalances.

Second, many Global South states emphasize meaningful human control and accountability. They argue that warfare must remain fundamentally connected to human responsibility and moral judgment.

Third, developing countries highlight the importance of preventing proliferation to non-state actors. The spread of inexpensive autonomous systems could create significant security challenges for states with limited technological resources.

However, emerging powers within the Global South also recognize the importance of technological development. Countries such as India, Brazil, the UAE, and South Africa seek governance frameworks that allow innovation while preventing irresponsible use.

Therefore, the Global South generally supports a balanced approach: stronger international regulation, preservation of human control, equitable technological access, and prevention of destabilizing autonomous warfare.
