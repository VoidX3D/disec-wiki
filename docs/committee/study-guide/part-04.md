# Part III-A: Major Powers (United States, China, Russia, United Kingdom, France)

> *PART III – COUNTRY STANCES AND BLOC POSITIONS*

France

France represents one of the most influential European voices in discussions concerning autonomous weapons and military artificial intelligence. As a permanent member of the United Nations Security Council, a nuclear-armed state, and one of Europe’s largest defence powers, France’s position carries significant diplomatic importance in shaping international approaches toward regulating emerging military technologies. French policy attempts to balance two competing objectives: preserving strategic autonomy and technological competitiveness while ensuring that artificial intelligence-enabled military systems remain consistent with international humanitarian law and ethical principles.

Unlike some states that advocate either unrestricted technological development or complete prohibition, France supports a regulatory approach centred on maintaining meaningful human involvement in decisions concerning the use of lethal force. The French government has repeatedly stated that human responsibility must remain central in military operations and that autonomous systems should not independently make decisions regarding the intentional targeting and destruction of human beings (French Ministry of Armed Forces, 2021).

France argues that the development of military AI is unavoidable, and that international security would be better served through responsible governance rather than absolute prohibition. Paris considers artificial intelligence a strategic technology essential for maintaining military effectiveness, particularly as global competition increasingly involves advanced technologies. French defence modernization programmes include AI applications in intelligence analysis, autonomous vehicles, cyber operations, battlefield management, satellite systems, and information warfare capabilities.

The French position is influenced significantly by European strategic culture, which traditionally emphasizes international law, multilateral institutions, and humanitarian considerations. France has supported discussions within the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW) regarding LAWS and has participated actively in developing international principles for responsible military AI. However, France has not supported proposals calling for an immediate comprehensive ban on autonomous weapons because it considers many autonomous technologies to have legitimate defensive and operational applications.

A key distinction in French policy is the separation between autonomous functions and autonomous lethal decision-making. France accepts the use of autonomy for tasks such as navigation, logistics, surveillance, and defensive systems, but it rejects the idea that machines should independently determine when and whom to kill. This approach reflects the broader European emphasis on preserving human judgment, accountability, and compliance with international humanitarian law.

France also recognizes the importance of technological sovereignty. As competition between the United States and China intensifies, European states increasingly seek independent capabilities in artificial intelligence, semiconductors, cybersecurity, and defence technologies. France views military AI development as necessary to prevent excessive dependence on external technological suppliers while maintaining European strategic autonomy (European Commission, 2024).

Nevertheless, France faces challenges in reconciling technological ambition with humanitarian commitments. Developing sophisticated AI capabilities requires substantial investment, access to advanced computing infrastructure, and cooperation between governments and private technology companies. France therefore supports international cooperation but seeks frameworks that regulate irresponsible uses rather than restrict technological innovation entirely.

The positions of the United States, China, Russia, the United Kingdom, and France demonstrate both common concerns and significant disagreements regarding the future governance of LAWS and military AI. Despite differences in political systems and strategic priorities, all five major powers recognize that artificial intelligence will increasingly influence future military operations. Each state acknowledges the importance of maintaining some degree of human responsibility, preventing accidental escalation, and ensuring compliance with international humanitarian law. However, their approaches diverge significantly regarding the appropriate level of international restriction.

The United States, United Kingdom, and France generally support a regulatory framework based on responsible use, transparency, testing, and human oversight. These countries argue that existing international humanitarian law remains applicable to emerging technologies and that additional regulations should avoid restricting legitimate defence innovation. Their position reflects their advanced military-industrial capabilities and interest in maintaining technological superiority.

China and Russia similarly resist comprehensive prohibitions but emphasize sovereignty and strategic independence more strongly. Both states argue that military AI development should not be constrained by regulations that could limit national security capabilities. While they support international discussions, they remain cautious about legally binding restrictions that could affect their technological development.

The main disagreement among these powers concerns whether international law requires a new legally binding instrument specifically addressing autonomous weapons. States supporting stronger restrictions argue that existing humanitarian law was created before the emergence of AI-driven warfare and therefore may not adequately address accountability, unpredictability, and machine decision-making. Conversely, states opposing new treaties argue that international humanitarian law is technology-neutral and already applies to all weapons systems.

Another major area of disagreement involves the definition of LAWS itself. Without a universally accepted definition, establishing regulations becomes extremely difficult. Some states define LAWS narrowly as weapons capable of independently selecting and engaging targets, while others include broader categories of autonomous systems. This definitional uncertainty has contributed to slow progress in international negotiations.

The positions of major powers therefore reveal a central tension within global governance: technological advancement is progressing faster than international regulation. States recognize the risks associated with autonomous warfare but remain reluctant to surrender strategic advantages in an increasingly competitive security environment.
