# Part III-C: Indo-Pacific and Asia

The Indo-Pacific region has emerged as one of the most significant theatres in the global development, deployment, and regulation of Military Artificial Intelligence and autonomous weapons technologies. The region contains several of the world’s largest military powers, rapidly developing economies, technologically advanced states, and strategically important maritime zones. Consequently, national approaches toward regulating Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS) are shaped not only by ethical and legal considerations but also by regional security competition, territorial disputes, alliance structures, defence modernization priorities, and concerns regarding technological dependency.

Unlike Europe, where discussions often emphasize humanitarian principles and legal restrictions, many Indo-Pacific states approach military AI through the lens of strategic competition and national security. The rise of China’s military capabilities, tensions in the Taiwan Strait, maritime disputes in the South China Sea, North Korea’s missile programme, and increasing competition between major powers have encouraged regional states to invest heavily in autonomous systems, artificial intelligence, robotics, cyber capabilities, and unmanned platforms. At the same time, many Asian countries recognize the importance of international norms, responsible innovation, and maintaining human control over lethal force.

India

India represents one of the most influential voices from the Global South and the Indo-Pacific region in discussions concerning military AI and autonomous weapons. As a rising technological power, a nuclear-armed state, and a major defence actor, India’s position reflects a combination of strategic modernization, technological ambition, and support for responsible international governance.

India views Artificial Intelligence as a transformative technology with significant implications for national security. The Indian Armed Forces have increasingly explored AI applications in areas such as intelligence analysis, autonomous surveillance, border monitoring, cybersecurity, predictive maintenance, and unmanned systems. Institutions such as the Defence Artificial Intelligence Council (DAIC) and the Defence AI Project Agency (DAIPA) demonstrate India’s commitment to integrating AI into defence capabilities (Ministry of Defence India, 2022).

India has not supported a complete prohibition of LAWS. Instead, New Delhi emphasizes the need for balanced international regulation that recognizes both security requirements and humanitarian concerns. India argues that discussions should focus on responsible use, accountability, and transparency rather than preventing technological development entirely.

India’s position reflects its strategic environment. Facing security challenges along its borders with China and Pakistan, India considers advanced military technologies essential for maintaining deterrence and operational effectiveness. Autonomous systems, AI-enabled surveillance, and unmanned platforms are viewed as important tools for improving defence capabilities, particularly in difficult geographical environments such as the Himalayas and maritime regions.

However, India also supports maintaining human responsibility in warfare. During international discussions, India has emphasized that machines should not independently determine matters involving the use of lethal force. India’s diplomatic position generally supports the continuation of international discussions under the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons while avoiding commitments toward restrictive treaties that could limit national security capabilities.

India’s approach therefore represents a middle position between technologically advanced states seeking flexibility and humanitarian groups demanding stronger restrictions. It advocates responsible innovation while preserving strategic autonomy.

Japan

Japan occupies a unique position in the Indo-Pacific due to its advanced technological capabilities, constitutional limitations on military force, alliance relationship with the United States, and growing security concerns in East Asia. Historically cautious regarding military technology because of its post-Second World War security identity, Japan has increasingly expanded defence modernization efforts due to regional challenges involving China and North Korea.

Japan recognizes Artificial Intelligence as an essential component of future defence capabilities. The Japanese Ministry of Defense has explored AI applications in intelligence, surveillance, autonomous vehicles, cybersecurity, information processing, and decision-support systems. Japan views AI as necessary for maintaining operational effectiveness while facing demographic limitations and increasing regional security challenges (Japan Ministry of Defense, 2023).

Japan generally supports responsible military AI development and emphasizes compliance with international humanitarian law. Tokyo does not advocate a complete ban on autonomous weapons but supports appropriate human involvement and accountability mechanisms. Japan argues that autonomous technologies can provide defensive benefits, including improving missile detection, maritime surveillance, and disaster-response capabilities.

Japan’s position is strongly influenced by its alliance with the United States. While Tokyo supports international regulation, it also recognizes the importance of maintaining technological compatibility with allied military systems. Therefore, Japan favours frameworks that promote responsible innovation rather than restrictive agreements that could create strategic disadvantages.

Republic of Korea (South Korea)

South Korea is among the most technologically advanced military powers in Asia and has invested heavily in AI-enabled defence systems due to its unique security environment involving North Korea. The constant military tension on the Korean Peninsula has encouraged Seoul to develop autonomous surveillance systems, unmanned vehicles, robotics, and AI-supported command capabilities.

South Korea’s defence industry has developed advanced autonomous technologies, including unmanned ground vehicles, surveillance drones, robotic systems, and AI-assisted battlefield management tools. The country considers military AI essential for improving operational efficiency and compensating for demographic challenges affecting military recruitment.

However, South Korea has also recognized the importance of responsible governance. Seoul supports international discussions on autonomous weapons and emphasizes that military AI should comply with international humanitarian law. Similar to other technologically advanced states, South Korea does not support a complete prohibition of autonomous systems because it considers AI essential for national defence.

The South Korean position demonstrates the dilemma faced by many technologically advanced states: while recognizing humanitarian concerns, security threats create strong incentives to continue military AI development.

Democratic People’s Republic of Korea (North Korea)

North Korea’s position regarding military AI and autonomous weapons is less transparent due to limited public information regarding its defence technology programmes. However, available evidence indicates increasing interest in unmanned systems, cyber capabilities, artificial intelligence, and advanced military technologies.

North Korea has demonstrated significant cyber capabilities and has invested heavily in asymmetric military technologies. While its conventional military technology may lag behind major powers, autonomous systems and cyber warfare provide opportunities to challenge technologically superior opponents at relatively lower costs.

The country’s approach is likely shaped by strategic deterrence against the United States and South Korea. Autonomous weapons, cyber capabilities, and AI-enabled systems could enhance North Korea’s ability to conduct surveillance, disrupt military networks, and improve battlefield effectiveness.

North Korea has historically been cautious toward international arms control agreements that could restrict its strategic capabilities. Therefore, it is unlikely to support regulations perceived as limiting technological development.

Pakistan

Pakistan approaches military AI through the perspective of regional security competition, particularly its strategic relationship with India. As a nuclear-armed state, Pakistan considers technological modernization essential for maintaining military balance.

Pakistan has explored AI applications through defence research institutions, focusing on areas such as autonomous systems, surveillance technologies, cybersecurity, and unmanned aerial vehicles. The country has developed experience with drone technology and recognizes the growing importance of AI-enabled warfare.

Pakistan generally supports international discussions on regulating autonomous weapons but does not support restrictions that could disadvantage developing states. Islamabad emphasizes equal access to technology, responsible use, and preventing monopolization of advanced military capabilities by major powers.

Pakistan’s position reflects broader Global South concerns that arms control frameworks should not create technological inequality between advanced and developing states.

Bangladesh and Nepal

Bangladesh and Nepal represent smaller South Asian states whose positions differ from major military powers due to limited defence resources and strategic priorities focused primarily on development, peacekeeping, and regional stability.

Bangladesh has traditionally emphasized peaceful diplomacy, United Nations peacekeeping, and responsible international security governance. While it does not possess significant autonomous weapons capabilities, Bangladesh recognizes the importance of preventing uncontrolled proliferation of emerging military technologies. Its approach generally aligns with humanitarian principles and supports international cooperation.

Nepal’s position is similarly shaped by its constitutional commitment to peace, non-alignment, and multilateral diplomacy. As a country with limited military technological capacity, Nepal is unlikely to pursue autonomous weapons development. Instead, Nepal’s priorities focus on ensuring that emerging military technologies do not increase global inequality, destabilize smaller states, or undermine international peace.

Both countries are likely to support transparency, international regulation, and humanitarian approaches toward autonomous weapons governance.

Singapore

Singapore represents one of Southeast Asia’s most technologically advanced defence actors. Despite its small geographical size, Singapore maintains sophisticated military capabilities and invests heavily in technology-driven defence modernization.

Singapore recognizes AI as essential for enhancing situational awareness, cybersecurity, intelligence, and operational efficiency. The country has developed advanced defence technology capabilities and supports responsible military AI development.

Singapore generally favours practical governance frameworks rather than prohibition. It emphasizes international cooperation, technological reliability, and maintaining human oversight in critical decisions.

Indonesia, Malaysia, and Vietnam

Southeast Asian states generally approach military AI through the perspectives of sovereignty, regional stability, and strategic autonomy. Indonesia, Malaysia, and Vietnam recognize the importance of emerging technologies but remain cautious about an arms race in the region.

Indonesia supports peaceful technological development and regional cooperation through ASEAN frameworks. Jakarta emphasizes that emerging military technologies should not undermine regional stability.

Malaysia similarly supports responsible AI governance while focusing on cybersecurity, maritime security, and defence modernization.

Vietnam’s position is influenced by regional tensions, particularly in the South China Sea. While Hanoi seeks advanced defence capabilities, it also supports international rules preventing destabilizing military competition.

ASEAN states generally prefer inclusive dialogue and confidence-building measures rather than confrontational arms control approaches.

Australia and New Zealand

Australia has become increasingly focused on military AI due to its alliance relationship with the United States and its strategic concerns in the Indo-Pacific. Through partnerships such as AUKUS, Australia is investing in advanced technologies including autonomous systems, artificial intelligence, cyber capabilities, and undersea technologies.

Australia supports responsible military AI and emphasizes compliance with international law. However, similar to other Western defence partners, it does not support complete prohibition of autonomous weapons.

New Zealand adopts a more humanitarian-oriented approach. As a smaller Pacific state with strong commitments to international law and arms control, New Zealand has expressed greater openness toward stronger restrictions on autonomous weapons. It emphasizes human control, accountability, and ethical considerations.

Indo-Pacific Strategic Analysis

The Indo-Pacific demonstrates the central contradiction of military AI governance. On one side, states recognize the humanitarian risks associated with autonomous weapons and support responsible international regulation. On the other side, regional security competition creates powerful incentives to continue developing advanced military technologies.

Major technological powers such as India, Japan, South Korea, Australia, and Singapore support regulation but resist restrictions that could limit defence modernization. Developing states such as Nepal and Bangladesh emphasize peaceful governance and preventing technological inequality. Strategic competitors such as China, India, and North Korea view AI through the lens of national security competition.

Therefore, the Indo-Pacific region is likely to remain one of the most important areas influencing the future governance of military AI and autonomous weapons.
