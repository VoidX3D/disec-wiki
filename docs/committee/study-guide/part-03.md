# Part II-B: Highlights to Key Words

> *PART III – COUNTRY STANCES AND BLOC POSITIONS*

A clear understanding of the terminology surrounding Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS) and Military Artificial Intelligence (AI) is fundamental for analysing the legal, ethical, technological, and geopolitical dimensions of the contemporary debate. As technological capabilities evolve more rapidly than international legal frameworks, the precise interpretation of key concepts has become increasingly important in diplomatic negotiations, military doctrine, and international law. The absence of universally accepted definitions for many of these terms has also complicated efforts within the framework of the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW) to establish legally binding regulations governing autonomous weapons. Consequently, understanding these concepts provides the analytical foundation necessary for evaluating state positions, policy proposals and future governance mechanisms.

Artificial Intelligence (AI) refers to computer systems designed to perform tasks that normally require human intelligence, including learning from data, recognising patterns, solving problems, understanding language, making predictions and supporting complex decision-making processes. Modern AI relies heavily on machine learning, deep learning and advanced computational algorithms that enable systems to improve their performance through experience rather than solely following explicitly programmed instructions. Within military contexts, AI has transformed intelligence analysis, surveillance, logistics, cyber defence, command-and-control systems and decision-support mechanisms. However, military AI encompasses a much broader spectrum than autonomous weapons alone, as many AI applications perform non-lethal support functions intended to improve operational efficiency while remaining under meaningful human supervision (Russell and Norvig, 2021).

Autonomy refers to the capability of a system to perform designated tasks independently after activation without requiring continuous human intervention. Unlike automated systems, which strictly follow predetermined instructions, autonomous systems are capable of perceiving their environment, processing incoming information, adapting to changing conditions and selecting appropriate responses within predefined operational parameters. The degree of autonomy exists along a spectrum ranging from low-level automation to full operational independence. In military operations, autonomy may involve navigation, obstacle avoidance, surveillance, target recognition, communication, or in more controversial cases, target selection and engagement. The increasing sophistication of autonomous capabilities has generated significant international concern because greater operational independence often reduces direct human oversight during critical battlefield decisions (Scharre, 2018).

Automation, although frequently confused with autonomy, represents a fundamentally different concept. Automated systems execute predefined rules and programmed instructions repeatedly without possessing the ability to learn, adapt or independently make judgments. Examples include conventional missile guidance systems, automated radar tracking, aircraft autopilot systems and defensive warning systems. These technologies increase efficiency and precision but remain constrained by deterministic programming established by human operators. In contrast, autonomous systems are capable of analysing dynamic environments and adjusting their behaviour according to changing operational circumstances, thereby introducing new legal and ethical considerations concerning accountability and human responsibility (United Nations Institute for Disarmament Research, 2023).

Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS) are generally understood as weapon systems capable of selecting and engaging targets with lethal force after activation without requiring further human intervention during the engagement process. Although no universally accepted legal definition currently exists, discussions within the CCW Group of Governmental Experts consistently focus on systems that combine target detection, identification, selection and attack using autonomous functions. The defining characteristic of LAWS is not simply the incorporation of artificial intelligence but rather the delegation of life-and-death decisions to machines with minimal or no real-time human control. This characteristic distinguishes LAWS from remotely piloted drones and conventional precision-guided munitions, where humans remain directly responsible for authorising the use of force (CCW Group of Governmental Experts, 2024).

Meaningful Human Control (MHC) has emerged as one of the central principles within international discussions concerning autonomous weapons. Although the concept has not yet received a universally binding legal definition, it broadly refers to ensuring that human operators retain sufficient involvement, understanding and authority over critical functions associated with the use of force. Meaningful human control requires that individuals possess adequate information regarding system capabilities, operational context, and potential consequences before authorising lethal actions. It also implies that humans remain capable of supervising, intervening and, where necessary, terminating autonomous operations. Many states consider meaningful human control indispensable for maintaining compliance with International Humanitarian Law, preserving moral responsibility, and ensuring legal accountability for battlefield decisions (Article 36, 2023).

Machine Learning (ML) constitutes one of the most influential technological developments underpinning modern military AI. Rather than relying exclusively upon explicitly programmed instructions, machine learning algorithms identify patterns from extensive datasets and improve predictive performance through iterative training. Military applications include object recognition, facial identification, cyber defence, intelligence analysis, predictive logistics, autonomous navigation and decision-support systems. Deep learning, a specialised subset of machine learning based upon artificial neural networks, has significantly enhanced the ability of AI systems to process satellite imagery, sensor data and complex battlefield environments with unprecedented speed and accuracy. Nevertheless, machine learning models may also produce unpredictable outputs, inherit biases from training datasets and exhibit limited transparency regarding how specific decisions are generated, thereby complicating legal review and operational accountability (Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville, 2016).

Human oversight over autonomous systems is frequently categorised into three operational models: Human-in-the-Loop, Human-on-the-Loop, and Human-out-of-the-Loop. Human-in-the-Loop systems require explicit human authorisation before lethal force is employed, ensuring direct human responsibility for engagement decisions. Human-on-the-Loop systems allow autonomous functions to operate independently while maintaining continuous human supervision, enabling operators to intervene or abort missions if necessary. Human-out-of-the-Loop systems represent the highest level of autonomy, permitting machines to independently detect, identify, select, and engage targets without real-time human approval once activated. This final category remains the most controversial because it raises profound concerns regarding accountability, ethical responsibility, civilian protection and compliance with International Humanitarian Law.

Target recognition refers to the technological capability of AI-enabled systems to identify and classify potential military objectives using computer vision, sensor fusion, radar signatures, thermal imaging, and behavioural analysis. Artificial intelligence compares observed objects against extensive datasets to distinguish combatants, military vehicles, infrastructure, and other operational targets. While these capabilities significantly enhance operational efficiency, no AI system currently demonstrates perfect reliability under all battlefield conditions. Factors such as environmental complexity, incomplete data, adversarial deception, and civilian presence may lead to erroneous classifications with potentially irreversible humanitarian consequences. Consequently, many experts argue that human judgement remains indispensable when determining the lawful use of lethal force (International Committee of the Red Cross, 2021).

Another increasingly significant concept is Swarm intelligence, which refers to the coordinated operation of multiple autonomous systems acting collectively without centralised control. Inspired by biological systems such as ant colonies, bee swarms and schools of fish, swarm algorithms enable numerous autonomous drones or robotic platforms to communicate, distribute tasks and adapt collectively to changing battlefield conditions. Swarming technologies offer considerable military advantages by increasing resilience, reducing operational costs and overwhelming conventional defensive systems through coordinated mass deployment. However, they also amplify concerns regarding escalation, attribution, command responsibility and civilian protection because large numbers of autonomous platforms may operate simultaneously across complex operational environments.

International Humanitarian Law (IHL), also known as the law of armed conflict, provides the principal legal framework governing the use of force during armed conflicts. The core principles of distinction, proportionality, military necessity, and precaution require parties to distinguish between civilians and combatants, avoid excessive civilian harm relative to anticipated military advantage and take all feasible precautions to minimise civilian casualties. Whether fully autonomous weapons can consistently satisfy these obligations remains one of the most contentious legal questions confronting the international community. Many states, legal scholars and humanitarian organisations maintain that meaningful human judgement remains indispensable for interpreting contextual battlefield information that artificial intelligence cannot reliably evaluate.

Finally, accountability represents perhaps the most challenging issue associated with LAWS. Traditional legal systems assign responsibility for unlawful acts to identifiable human actors, including military commanders, political leaders, and weapon operators. Autonomous weapons complicate this framework because decision-making may involve software developers, manufacturers, commanders, procurement authorities and machine-learning algorithms operating simultaneously. Determining who should bear criminal, civil or state responsibility following unlawful autonomous attacks remains unresolved under contemporary international law. This accountability gap has become a central justification advanced by states and international organisations advocating either legally binding regulation or preventive prohibition of fully autonomous weapons. As artificial intelligence continues to evolve, the establishment of universally accepted definitions and governance mechanisms for these key concepts will remain essential for balancing technological innovation with humanitarian protection, international security, and the rule of law.
