Introduction to the Committee

The Disarmament and International Security Committee (DISEC), formally recognized as the First Committee of the United Nations General Assembly (UNGA), serves as the principal international forum dedicated to addressing global challenges associated with international peace, collective security, arms control, disarmament, and emerging military technologies. Since its establishment in 1945 following the creation of the United Nations, DISEC has played an indispensable role in facilitating dialogue among sovereign states on matters that directly or indirectly threaten international stability. The committee functions as the primary venue where Member States deliberate on issues relating to conventional weapons, nuclear disarmament, outer space security, cyber warfare, terrorism involving weapons of mass destruction, confidence-building measures, military transparency, and the governance of emerging defence technologies such as Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS) (United Nations, 2025).

The creation of DISEC was rooted in the catastrophic consequences of the Second World War, which demonstrated the devastating humanitarian, political, and economic impacts of modern warfare. The unprecedented destruction caused by nuclear weapons at Hiroshima and Nagasaki fundamentally altered the international community's understanding of warfare, highlighting the necessity of establishing multilateral institutions capable of preventing future conflicts. Consequently, the Charter of the United Nations enshrined the maintenance of international peace and security as one of the organization's primary purposes under Article 1, while Article 11 specifically empowered the General Assembly to consider general principles governing disarmament and arms regulation (United Nations, 1945).

Unlike executive organs such as the United Nations Security Council, DISEC does not possess enforcement authority or legally binding decision-making powers. Instead, its significance lies in norm creation, consensus-building, policy formulation, and the promotion of international cooperation. The committee adopts resolutions, recommendations, declarations, and studies that frequently influence subsequent treaty negotiations, international conventions, and national defence policies.

Although General Assembly resolutions are generally non-binding under international law, they often carry substantial political, diplomatic, and normative weight. Many landmark international arms control agreements including the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of nuclear weapons (NPT), the Chemical Weapons Convention (CWC), the Biological Weapons Convention (BWC), the Arms Trade Treaty (ATT), and discussions surrounding the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW)have been shaped by extensive deliberations within the First Committee (UNODA, 2024).

The scope of DISEC has expanded considerably since the Cold War. Initially focused primarily on nuclear disarmament and strategic arms reduction between major military powers, the committee now addresses a diverse array of security challenges arising from rapid technological advancement, globalization, transnational terrorism, hybrid warfare, cyber operations, autonomous military systems, and the militarization of emerging domains such as outer space and cyberspace. These developments have transformed international security from being centred solely on state-to-state military competition into a multidimensional environment involving non-state actors, private technology companies, autonomous systems, and digital infrastructure (SIPRI, 2025).

One defining characteristic of DISEC is its emphasis on preventive diplomacy. Rather than responding to conflicts after they occur, the committee seeks to identify emerging threats before they destabilize international peace. Preventive diplomacy includes confidence-building measures, transparency initiatives, military information sharing, arms control negotiations, and the development of international norms governing responsible state behaviour. This forward-looking approach is particularly important in the context of military AI, where technological innovation is progressing faster than international legal and regulatory frameworks.

The committee operates under the fundamental principles of sovereign equality, multilateralism, peaceful settlement of disputes, and collective security. Every Member State of the United Nations, regardless of its political system, economic strength, or military capability, possesses one vote within the General Assembly. This institutional design reflects the principle that global security is a shared responsibility requiring inclusive participation. Consequently, DISEC provides smaller and developing nations with opportunities to influence international security discourse alongside major military powers.

Over the past decade, technological innovation has significantly reshaped the committee's priorities. Artificial Intelligence, machine learning, autonomous robotics, quantum computing, hypersonic weapons, cyber capabilities, and advanced surveillance technologies are increasingly integrated into national defence strategies. These technologies promise enhanced military efficiency, improved situational awareness, and reduced risks to military personnel. Simultaneously, however, they introduce unprecedented ethical, legal, operational, and strategic challenges. The prospect of machines independently selecting and engaging human targets raises profound questions regarding accountability, compliance with international humanitarian law, proportionality, distinction, and meaningful human control.

Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems represent one of the most controversial technological developments currently confronting the international community. Unlike remotely operated drones, autonomous weapon systems are designed to perceive their operational environment, identify targets, evaluate threats, and execute lethal force with varying degrees of human intervention. Advances in computer vision, neural networks, sensor fusion, reinforcement learning, and edge computing have accelerated the feasibility of such systems.

Several technologically advanced states including the United States, China, Russia, Israel, South Korea, Türkiye, and members of the European Unionare actively investing in AI-enabled military capabilities, although their approaches toward international regulation differ considerably (UNIDIR, 2023).

The growing integration of AI into military operations extends well beyond autonomous weapons. Modern armed forces increasingly employ AI for intelligence analysis, predictive logistics, cyber defence, battlefield surveillance, autonomous navigation, decision support systems, electronic warfare, and command-and-control optimization. While many of these applications remain under human supervision, continued advances in autonomy raise concerns that future systems could progressively reduce meaningful human involvement in decisions concerning the use of lethal force. This evolving reality has intensified international debates regarding appropriate governance mechanisms.

DISEC serves as the principal multilateral forum where these debates are conducted. Discussions encompass not only technical aspects of AI but also broader legal, ethical, humanitarian, strategic, and geopolitical implications. States differ substantially in their perspectives. Some advocate a legally binding international treaty prohibiting autonomous weapons lacking meaningful human control, arguing that such systems violate fundamental principles of humanity and international humanitarian law. Others maintain that existing international law already provides sufficient regulation and caution against restrictions that could hinder legitimate technological innovation or national security interests. These differing viewpoints illustrate the complex balance between humanitarian concerns and military necessity.

The committee also collaborates closely with numerous United Nations entities and specialized international organizations. The United Nations Office for Disarmament Affairs (UNODA) supports negotiations on arms control and disarmament initiatives. The United Nations Institute for Disarmament Research (UNIDIR) conducts technical and policy research on emerging security technologies. The International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC) contributes expertise concerning the application of international humanitarian law to autonomous weapons. Additionally, the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW) serves as the primary legal framework within which governmental experts continue discussions on LAWS, often informed by debates occurring in DISEC (ICRC, 2024).

The committee's relevance has increased further as military AI becomes intertwined with broader geopolitical competition. The strategic rivalry among major powers including the United States, China, Russia, and increasingly technologically capable middle powers has transformed AI into a critical determinant of future military superiority. Defence expenditure on AI research has grown substantially worldwide, with governments investing heavily in autonomous drones, unmanned naval systems, robotic ground vehicles, AI-assisted missile defence, autonomous logistics, and intelligent command systems. These developments risk triggering an international AI arms race characterized by accelerated technological competition, reduced transparency, and diminished strategic stability (IISS, 2025).

Another emerging concern involves the accessibility of AI technologies beyond traditional state actors. Commercially available machine learning models, open-source software, low-cost drones, and increasingly affordable computing resources have lowered barriers to entry for non-state actors.

Terrorist organizations, criminal networks, and private military companies may exploit AI-enabled capabilities for surveillance, autonomous attacks, cyber operations, or misinformation campaigns. Consequently, DISEC increasingly considers not only interstate competition, but also broader challenges related to technological proliferation, export controls, dual-use research, and international governance.

The committee also recognizes the intersection between military AI and sustainable global development. Excessive military competition diverts financial resources away from healthcare, education, climate resilience, poverty reduction, and sustainable development initiatives. Conversely, responsible governance of emerging military technologies may contribute to international stability, confidence-building, and peaceful scientific cooperation. This broader perspective aligns with the United Nations' commitment to maintaining peace while promoting sustainable human development.

Ultimately, DISEC occupies a unique position within the international security architecture. It functions not as a battlefield commander or judicial authority but as a global platform where diplomacy, international law, technological innovation, humanitarian principles, and national security interests converge. Its discussions influence the evolution of international norms governing warfare and shape the legal and ethical standards that future military technologies must satisfy. As Artificial Intelligence continues to redefine the nature of armed conflict, the committee's role in fostering dialogue, promoting responsible state behaviour, and developing internationally accepted frameworks for regulating autonomous weapons will become increasingly central to maintaining global peace, security, and human dignity.

Introduction to the Agenda

Regulating Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS) and Military Artificial Intelligence

The rapid advancement of Artificial Intelligence (AI) has fundamentally transformed numerous aspects of modern society, including healthcare, transportation, finance, manufacturing, education, and communication. Increasingly, however, AI has become an integral component of national defence and military modernization strategies. Governments across the world are investing unprecedented resources into AI-enabled military capabilities, ranging from autonomous reconnaissance drones and decision-support systems to intelligent missile defence networks, robotic ground vehicles, autonomous naval platforms, and increasingly sophisticated weapons capable of identifying, tracking, and engaging targets with varying degrees of human intervention. These developments have introduced one of the most consequential security debates of the twenty-first century: how should the international community regulate Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS) and the broader integration of Artificial Intelligence into military operations while balancing technological innovation, national security, humanitarian principles, and international law (UNIDIR, 2023).

Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems represent a significant departure from conventional weapons because they incorporate computational intelligence capable of performing tasks that were historically reserved for human operators. Although definitions remain contested internationally, LAWS are generally understood as weapon systems that, once activated, can independently perceive their operational environment, identify potential targets, evaluate available information, select an object or individual for engagement, and employ lethal force with limited or no additional human intervention (ICRC, 2021). Unlike remotely piloted drones, which remain continuously controlled by human operators, autonomous weapons possess varying degrees of independent decision-making capacity based on algorithms, sensor inputs, machine learning models, and real-time environmental analysis.

The emergence of such technologies has blurred the traditional distinction between human judgment and machine execution in warfare. Historically, decisions regarding the lawful use of lethal force have relied upon human reasoning, ethical judgment, military training, situational awareness, and legal accountability. Military commanders evaluate proportionality, distinction, military necessity, precaution, and humanitarian considerations before authorizing attacks. AI-enabled autonomous systems challenge these longstanding assumptions by introducing algorithms into decisions that may ultimately determine life or death. Consequently, the debate surrounding LAWS extends beyond engineering or military efficiency; it encompasses profound legal, ethical, political, strategic, and humanitarian questions concerning the future conduct of armed conflict (Boulanin and Verbruggen, 2017).

The integration of Artificial Intelligence into military affairs is not limited to autonomous weapons alone. Modern armed forces increasingly utilize AI across nearly every operational domain. Intelligence agencies employ machine learning algorithms to analyse satellite imagery, detect troop movements, process vast quantities of surveillance data, and identify potential threats. Logistics commands use predictive analytics to optimize supply chains, maintenance schedules, and resource allocation. Cyber defence systems rely upon AI to detect malicious intrusions, automate incident response, and strengthen digital resilience.

Naval forces are developing autonomous underwater vehicles capable of long-duration surveillance, while air forces employ AI-assisted mission planning and target recognition. These applications illustrate that military AI encompasses an extensive technological ecosystem extending well beyond autonomous weapons (Scharre, 2018).

Nevertheless, LAWS remain the focal point of international concern because they directly involve the autonomous application of lethal force. This raises complex questions regarding compliance with International Humanitarian Law (IHL), particularly the principles of distinction, proportionality, military necessity, and precaution. The principle of distinction requires parties to an armed conflict to differentiate between combatants and civilians, targeting only legitimate military objectives. Proportionality prohibits attacks expected to cause excessive civilian harm relative to the anticipated military advantage. Precaution obliges military planners to minimize incidental civilian casualties whenever feasible. Whether autonomous systems can consistently satisfy these legal obligations remains one of the most contested issues in contemporary international law (ICRC, 2021).

Closely connected to legal concerns are questions of accountability. If an autonomous weapon unlawfully kills civilians, determining responsibility becomes considerably more complicated than in traditional military operations. Accountability may potentially involve military commanders, software engineers, system manufacturers, defence contractors, government procurement authorities, political leaders, or algorithm developers. Existing legal frameworks were primarily designed around human decision-makers rather than autonomous computational systems. Consequently, assigning criminal, civil, or state responsibility for algorithmic decisions presents significant legal uncertainty. This phenomenon is frequently described as the "accountability gap," referring to situations in which responsibility becomes diffused across multiple actors without a clearly identifiable decision-maker (Human Rights Watch, 2020).

Ethical considerations further intensify the debate. Numerous scholars, humanitarian organizations, religious leaders, and civil society groups argue that delegating decisions concerning human life to machines undermines fundamental principles of human dignity and moral responsibility. The concept of meaningful human control has therefore emerged as a central normative principle within international discussions. Although no universally accepted definition currently exists, meaningful human control generally requires that humans retain sufficient involvement in decisions concerning the selection and engagement of targets, ensuring that lethal force remains subject to human judgment rather than purely algorithmic processes (UNODA, 2024). Advocates contend that preserving meaningful human control is essential for maintaining both legal accountability and ethical legitimacy in warfare.

At the same time, proponents of military AI emphasize its potential operational advantages. AI systems can process information significantly faster than humans, analyse vast datasets in real time, reduce operator fatigue, enhance precision targeting, improve situational awareness, and perform dangerous missions without exposing military personnel to direct risk. Autonomous mine-clearing robots, explosive ordnance disposal systems, defensive anti-missile platforms, and search-and-rescue drones demonstrate that autonomous technologies may generate important humanitarian and military benefits when appropriately designed and employed. Some states therefore argue that prohibiting autonomous weapons entirely could impede technological innovation and deny militaries capabilities capable of reducing unintended casualties under certain operational circumstances (Department of Defense, 2023).

The strategic implications of military AI have also become increasingly significant within contemporary geopolitics. Artificial Intelligence is widely regarded as a critical determinant of future economic competitiveness, technological leadership, and military superiority. Major powers including the United States, China, Russia, members of the European Union, Israel, the Republic of Korea, India, and Türkiye have adopted national AI strategies incorporating substantial defence investments. Competition over AI increasingly resembles previous strategic competitions involving nuclear weapons, missile technology, and cyber capabilities, although AI differs because many underlying technologies possess extensive civilian applications. This dual-use nature complicates regulation, as restrictions affecting military AI may also influence commercial innovation, scientific research, and economic development (OECD, 2024).

International debates concerning LAWS primarily occur within the framework of the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW), where the Group of Governmental Experts (GGE) on LAWS has convened regularly since 2017. Participating states continue to examine legal definitions, technological developments, ethical considerations, and potential regulatory options. However, substantial disagreements remain regarding whether existing international humanitarian law is sufficient, whether legally binding prohibitions should be negotiated, or whether politically binding principles and best practices provide a more appropriate regulatory approach (United Nations, 2025). These divergent positions reflect broader geopolitical differences regarding military modernization, national security priorities, technological competitiveness, and interpretations of international law.

Another factor contributing to the urgency of this agenda is the accelerating pace of technological development. Advances in deep learning, computer vision, natural language processing, reinforcement learning, autonomous navigation, edge computing, and sensor fusion continue to enhance the capabilities of AI-enabled military systems. Commercial AI innovation increasingly influences defence applications, while private technology companies have become central actors in military research and development. The widespread availability of open-source AI models, inexpensive drones, cloud computing infrastructure, and advanced semiconductor technologies has lowered barriers to entry, enabling both state and non-state actors to acquire increasingly sophisticated capabilities. Consequently, concerns extend beyond traditional interstate warfare to include terrorism, organized crime, cyber-enabled attacks, and autonomous weapons proliferation.

The contemporary security environment further reinforces the significance of regulating military AI. Recent armed conflicts have demonstrated the expanding role of autonomous and AI-assisted systems in intelligence gathering, surveillance, target identification, electronic warfare, cyber operations, and drone combat. Although fully autonomous lethal systems remain relatively limited, ongoing conflicts illustrate the trajectory toward progressively greater autonomy in military operations. The integration of AI into battlefield decision-making has accelerated military effectiveness but has simultaneously intensified debates regarding escalation risks, algorithmic bias, accidental engagements, misidentification of civilians, cybersecurity vulnerabilities, and the possibility of autonomous systems interacting unpredictably in highly dynamic combat environments (SIPRI, 2025).

Beyond armed conflict, military AI also influences international strategic stability. Autonomous systems capable of operating at machine speed may compress decision-making timelines during crises, increasing the likelihood of miscalculation and unintended escalation between nuclear-armed states.

AI-enabled early warning systems, autonomous cyber responses, and algorithm-driven command support may inadvertently amplify rather than reduce strategic instability if insufficient human oversight exists. These concerns have prompted growing attention to confidence-building measures, transparency initiatives, verification mechanisms, and responsible AI governance within international security discussions.

The regulation of LAWS and military AI therefore represents one of the defining governance challenges of the modern international system. Unlike previous arms control debates focused primarily on physical weapons, this agenda involves software, algorithms, data, sensors, computing infrastructure, and rapidly evolving technologies that often originate within civilian industries before being adapted for military purposes. Effective governance must therefore reconcile technological innovation with humanitarian protection, military necessity with ethical responsibility, and national sovereignty with collective international security.

Ultimately, the discussion surrounding LAWS and military AI is not solely about whether autonomous technologies should exist. Rather, it concerns establishing internationally accepted principles governing how, under what conditions, and to what extent artificial intelligence may participate in decisions involving the use of lethal force. The choices made today will profoundly influence the future character of warfare, the protection of civilian populations, the evolution of international humanitarian law, and the maintenance of international peace and security in an increasingly autonomous world.
