# PART VI – INTERNATIONAL TREATIES AND AGREEMENTS AND OTHER INTERNATIONAL AGENCIES’ EFFORTS TO FOSTER THE AGENDA INTO PRACTICE

International Legal Frameworks, Diplomatic Mechanisms, and Institutional Efforts for Governing Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems and Military Artificial Intelligence

The emergence of Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS) and Military Artificial Intelligence has created a new challenge for the international security system because existing arms control mechanisms were primarily designed to regulate physical weapons rather than intelligent systems capable of processing information, adapting to environments, and making increasingly independent decisions. Consequently, international efforts to govern autonomous weapons have focused on interpreting existing international humanitarian law, developing new diplomatic frameworks, establishing ethical principles, and creating confidence-building mechanisms among states.

Although no legally binding international treaty specifically prohibiting or comprehensively regulating LAWS currently exists, numerous international organizations, states, and expert communities have developed frameworks intended to ensure that military AI remains consistent with humanitarian principles and international security objectives. These efforts demonstrate a gradual movement toward establishing global norms, although significant disagreements remain regarding whether future regulation should involve prohibition, restriction, or responsible-use frameworks.

Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW)

The Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW) represents the most important international forum addressing autonomous weapons. Adopted in 1980, the CCW seeks to prohibit or restrict weapons considered excessively injurious or indiscriminate. The Convention has historically addressed weapons such as incendiary weapons, blinding laser weapons, mines, and explosive remnants of war.

The relevance of the CCW to autonomous weapons emerged because LAWS raised concerns regarding the principles of distinction, proportionality, and unnecessary suffering under international humanitarian law. Unlike traditional weapons, autonomous systems introduce uncertainty regarding how machines identify targets, evaluate situations, and execute decisions.

In 2013, states participating in the CCW began discussing autonomous weapons through informal expert meetings. These discussions expanded in 2016 with the establishment of the Group of Governmental Experts (GGE) on LAWS, which became the primary international diplomatic mechanism for examining the implications of autonomous weapons.

The CCW process has created important areas of consensus. States generally agree that international humanitarian law applies to all weapons systems, including emerging autonomous technologies. There is also broad agreement that accountability for military operations must remain with humans and that weapons systems require appropriate testing, verification, and operational control.

However, the CCW process has faced limitations due to disagreement among states regarding the scope of regulation. Countries such as Austria, Mexico, Brazil, and several humanitarian-oriented states have advocated stronger legal restrictions, while countries including the United States, Russia, and China have generally preferred non-binding guidelines and responsible-use frameworks.

The inability to achieve consensus demonstrates the broader challenge of regulating technologies that have both civilian and military applications.

Group of Governmental Experts (GGE) on Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems

The establishment of the GGE on LAWS represented a major international effort to create dialogue between governments, military experts, legal scholars, and civil society representatives. Operating under the CCW framework, the GGE examines technological developments, ethical concerns, legal implications, and possible regulatory approaches.

The GGE discussions have produced several guiding principles that shape current international debates. These principles include the responsibility of humans for decisions involving the use of force, the requirement that autonomous systems comply with international humanitarian law, and the importance of maintaining accountability throughout the lifecycle of weapons systems.

The GGE has also emphasized the importance of human-machine interaction rather than focusing exclusively on whether a weapon is autonomous. This approach recognizes that autonomy exists along a spectrum, ranging from systems requiring constant human control to systems capable of operating independently under certain conditions.

Despite these achievements, the GGE has not produced a legally binding agreement. The primary obstacle remains disagreement regarding whether autonomous weapons should be prohibited or regulated.

International Humanitarian Law and the Geneva Convention Framework

International Humanitarian Law (IHL) remains the central legal foundation for discussions regarding autonomous weapons. Developed through treaties such as the Geneva Conventions of 1949 and their Additional Protocols, IHL establishes rules designed to limit suffering during armed conflict and protect civilians.

Several fundamental principles are particularly relevant to LAWS.

The principle of distinction requires combatants to differentiate between military targets and civilians. Autonomous systems must therefore possess sufficient technological capability to identify legitimate targets accurately. Critics argue that current AI systems may struggle in complex environments where civilian and military objects are difficult to distinguish.

The principle of proportionality prohibits attacks where expected civilian harm would be excessive compared with anticipated military advantage. Unlike simple identification tasks, proportionality requires contextual judgment involving moral, strategic, and legal considerations. This raises questions regarding whether autonomous systems can perform such assessments reliably.

The principle of precaution requires parties to armed conflicts to take feasible measures to minimize civilian harm. This creates obligations regarding weapon design, deployment, and operational control.

The application of these principles demonstrates that existing humanitarian law remains relevant but may require additional interpretation to address autonomous technologies.

Geneva Conventions and Human Responsibility

Although the Geneva Conventions do not specifically mention autonomous weapons, their underlying principles provide an important framework for evaluating emerging technologies. The central idea of human responsibility in warfare remains fundamental.

Humanitarian organizations argue that allowing machines to independently determine targets could weaken accountability because traditional legal systems rely on identifying responsible individuals. If an autonomous system causes unlawful harm, determining responsibility among commanders, programmers, manufacturers, and operators becomes complicated.

The International Committee of the Red Cross has emphasized that human judgment must remain central in decisions involving life and death. The organization argues that maintaining meaningful human control is essential for preserving legal accountability and ethical responsibility (ICRC, 2021).

Arms Trade Treaty (ATT) and Technology Transfer Concerns

The Arms Trade Treaty (ATT), adopted in 2013, regulates international transfers of conventional weapons and seeks to prevent irresponsible arms transfers that contribute to human rights violations and conflict.

Although the ATT does not directly regulate autonomous weapons or artificial intelligence, its principles are relevant because future autonomous systems may involve international transfers of drones, robotics, sensors, and AI-enabled military technologies.

The ATT emphasizes risk assessment before transferring weapons, including consideration of potential humanitarian consequences. These principles may become increasingly important as autonomous technologies become more widely available.

However, the ATT faces limitations because many AI components are dual-use technologies also used in civilian industries. Regulating AI transfers therefore presents greater challenges than regulating traditional weapons.

United Nations General Assembly and International Discussions

The United Nations General Assembly has increasingly addressed the broader implications of artificial intelligence for international peace and security. Discussions have focused on ensuring that emerging technologies contribute to peace rather than intensify conflict.

UN bodies have emphasized the importance of international cooperation, ethical governance, transparency, and preventing technological inequality. The United Nations Secretary-General has repeatedly highlighted the need for global frameworks addressing the risks associated with autonomous weapons and artificial intelligence.

The UN’s role remains particularly important because it provides a platform where states with different technological capabilities can participate equally in discussions.

United Nations Institute for Disarmament Research (UNIDIR)

UNIDIR has made significant contributions to understanding autonomous weapons through research, expert discussions, and policy analysis. The organization examines technological developments, security implications, and possible governance mechanisms.

UNIDIR has emphasized that regulation must consider not only the technical capabilities of autonomous systems but also broader issues including strategic stability, proliferation, accountability, and human control.

Its research has helped policymakers understand the complexity of regulating AI-enabled weapons.

International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC)

The ICRC has been one of the most influential humanitarian organizations involved in autonomous weapons discussions. Its position emphasizes that human control must remain central in decisions regarding the use of force.

The ICRC argues that autonomous weapons create three major concerns: difficulties in predicting their behaviour, challenges in ensuring accountability, and ethical concerns regarding delegating lethal decisions to machines.

The organization has called for international rules ensuring that autonomous weapons cannot operate without meaningful human involvement.

OECD AI Principles and UNESCO AI Ethics Framework

Although primarily designed for civilian artificial intelligence governance, international AI ethics frameworks developed by organizations such as the OECD and UNESCO have influenced discussions regarding military AI.

The OECD AI Principles emphasize transparency, robustness, security, accountability, and human-centred development. UNESCO’s Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence highlights human rights, dignity, fairness, and responsibility.

While these frameworks do not directly regulate military applications, they contribute to broader international thinking regarding responsible AI governance.

NATO Artificial Intelligence Strategy

NATO has developed its own framework for responsible military AI development. The NATO Artificial Intelligence Strategy identifies principles including lawfulness, responsibility, explainability, reliability, governability, and bias mitigation.

The strategy recognizes AI as essential for future defence capabilities while emphasizing that technological development must remain consistent with democratic values and international law.

NATO’s approach reflects the broader position of many technologically advanced states: supporting regulation while avoiding restrictions that could limit military innovation.
