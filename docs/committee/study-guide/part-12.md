# PART VII – PRESENTING THE ACTUAL PROBLEMS IN HAND

Core Challenges and Contemporary Dilemmas Associated with Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems and Military Artificial Intelligence

The development of Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS) and Military Artificial Intelligence represents one of the most complex security challenges of the twenty-first century because it combines technological innovation with fundamental questions regarding warfare, human responsibility, international law, and global stability. Unlike traditional weapons systems, autonomous weapons introduce a new dimension where machines may independently analyse information, identify potential targets, make operational decisions, and execute actions with limited or delayed human involvement. This transformation creates challenges that extend beyond military effectiveness and enter the domains of ethics, law, diplomacy, cybersecurity, and international peace.

The central problem is that technological advancement is progressing faster than international governance mechanisms. While artificial intelligence capabilities continue to improve rapidly, the international community has not yet developed universally accepted standards defining acceptable levels of autonomy, human involvement, accountability, and responsibility. This regulatory gap creates uncertainty regarding how autonomous systems should be controlled and who should be responsible when these systems cause unintended consequences.

1. Accountability Gap and Responsibility Dilemma

One of the most significant challenges associated with LAWS is the question of accountability. In traditional warfare, responsibility for decisions involving the use of force can generally be traced through a chain of command. Military commanders, operators, and political authorities are accountable for decisions made during armed conflict.

However, autonomous weapons complicate this traditional accountability structure because decision-making processes may involve algorithms, machine-learning models, software developers, manufacturers, commanders, and operators. When an autonomous system makes an incorrect decision resulting in civilian casualties or unlawful harm, determining responsibility becomes extremely difficult.

This problem is often referred to as the “accountability gap.” If a machine independently identifies and attacks a target, questions arise regarding who should bear legal responsibility: the commander who deployed the system, the programmer who designed the algorithm, the company that produced the technology, or the military institution that authorized its use.

The International Committee of the Red Cross has highlighted that maintaining human responsibility is essential because international humanitarian law is based on the principle that humans, not machines, are responsible for decisions during armed conflict (ICRC, 2021). Without clear accountability mechanisms, autonomous weapons could weaken existing legal frameworks and create situations where unlawful actions occur without clearly identifiable responsibility.

2. Human Control and the Delegation of Lethal Decisions

The question of meaningful human control represents one of the central debates surrounding autonomous weapons. The fundamental concern is whether humans should retain direct involvement in decisions involving the use of lethal force.

Supporters of autonomous technologies argue that AI systems can improve military effectiveness, reduce human error, and potentially decrease civilian casualties by improving accuracy and reaction speed. They argue that human operators may continue supervising autonomous systems through carefully designed command structures.

However, critics argue that delegating life-and-death decisions to machines creates serious ethical concerns. Human judgment involves understanding context, interpreting intentions, considering moral consequences, and applying legal principles. AI systems, despite advanced computational abilities, do not possess human consciousness, empathy, or moral reasoning.

The concept of meaningful human control has therefore become a central principle in international discussions. It suggests that humans must maintain sufficient involvement throughout the lifecycle of autonomous weapons, including development, deployment, supervision, and decision-making.

The challenge lies in defining what level of human involvement is sufficient. Different states interpret human control differently, creating difficulties in establishing international standards.

3. Compliance with International Humanitarian Law

A major legal challenge is whether autonomous weapons can reliably comply with international humanitarian law (IHL). Existing humanitarian law requires armed forces to distinguish between combatants and civilians, ensure proportionality between military advantage and civilian harm, and take precautions during attacks.

Autonomous systems may face difficulties applying these principles because warfare often occurs in unpredictable environments involving complex human behaviour.

The principle of distinction requires recognizing legitimate military targets. While AI systems may process large amounts of visual and sensor data, distinguishing between combatants and civilians in real-world situations remains extremely challenging.

For example, a person carrying equipment may be a soldier, civilian, medical worker, or someone attempting self-defence. Such distinctions require contextual understanding that remains difficult for artificial intelligence.

Similarly, proportionality assessments require evaluating whether expected civilian harm is excessive compared with anticipated military advantage. This involves ethical and strategic judgment rather than simple calculation.

These challenges raise concerns about whether autonomous systems can meet the legal standards required under international humanitarian law.

4. Risk to Civilian Protection and Human Security

One of the strongest arguments against uncontrolled autonomous weapons development is the potential risk to civilian populations. Modern conflicts increasingly occur in urban environments where military forces operate among civilian communities.

Autonomous systems operating in such environments may struggle to differentiate between legitimate military targets and civilians. Errors in identification, software malfunction, unexpected environmental conditions, or inaccurate data could result in unintended casualties.

Furthermore, autonomous weapons could reduce the political and psychological barriers associated with the use of military force. Because autonomous systems reduce risks to soldiers, governments may become more willing to initiate military operations.

This possibility creates concerns that autonomous warfare could make conflicts more frequent and easier to escalate.

Humanitarian organizations argue that civilian protection must remain the primary consideration in regulating autonomous weapons. They emphasize that technological efficiency cannot replace the ethical obligation to protect human life.

5. Algorithmic Bias and Discrimination Risks

Artificial intelligence systems rely on data for training and decision-making. However, data can contain inaccuracies, limitations, and biases. When military AI systems are trained on incomplete or biased information, they may produce discriminatory or inaccurate outcomes.

For example, an AI-based identification system may perform differently depending on geographic location, environmental conditions, ethnicity-related visual characteristics, or available training data. Such biases could disproportionately affect certain populations.

Unlike human soldiers, who may recognize uncertainty and adjust decisions based on experience, autonomous systems may repeat systematic errors at greater speed and scale.

Algorithmic bias therefore represents not only a technological problem but also a humanitarian and legal concern.

6. Cybersecurity Vulnerabilities and System Manipulation

Autonomous weapons depend heavily on software, communication networks, sensors, and data systems. This dependence creates significant cybersecurity risks.

Military AI systems may be vulnerable to hacking, data manipulation, electronic warfare, or adversarial attacks. An opponent could attempt to deceive an autonomous system by altering data inputs, disrupting communication networks, or manipulating algorithms.

For example, a cyberattack could cause an autonomous system to misidentify targets, malfunction, or become controlled by hostile actors.

The cybersecurity challenge is particularly significant because autonomous weapons may operate at speeds beyond human reaction capabilities. A compromised system could therefore create rapid and unpredictable consequences.

Ensuring cybersecurity, reliability, and resilience is essential before deploying advanced autonomous systems.

7. Proliferation and Accessibility Risks

Another major concern is the potential spread of autonomous weapons technology. Unlike nuclear weapons, which require enormous financial resources and specialized infrastructure, many AI-enabled systems rely on commercially available technologies.

Advances in drones, robotics, sensors, and artificial intelligence could allow smaller states or non-state actors to acquire increasingly sophisticated capabilities.

Terrorist organizations, criminal groups, or insurgent movements could potentially modify commercially available autonomous technologies for harmful purposes.

The proliferation of autonomous weapons therefore creates challenges for international security because traditional arms control mechanisms may not effectively regulate technologies with civilian applications.

8. AI Arms Race and Strategic Instability

The development of military AI has created concerns regarding a new technological arms race. Major powers increasingly view artificial intelligence as a strategic advantage comparable to previous revolutionary military technologies.

Competition among the United States, China, Russia, and other technologically advanced states may encourage rapid development without sufficient consideration of humanitarian consequences.

## An AI arms race could create several risks

First, states may prioritize speed over safety in order to avoid strategic disadvantage.

Second, increasing automation may reduce decision-making time during conflicts, increasing the possibility of accidental escalation.

Third, countries with limited technological capabilities may feel pressured to develop autonomous systems despite lacking sufficient regulatory structures.

Strategic stability therefore represents one of the most important challenges associated with military AI.

9. Lack of Common Definition and Regulatory Standards

A major obstacle in international negotiations is the absence of a universally accepted definition of LAWS.

Different states define autonomy differently. Some consider a system autonomous only if it independently selects and attacks targets, while others include systems with advanced decision-support capabilities.

Without a common definition, creating effective regulations becomes extremely difficult.

Similarly, states disagree regarding whether regulation should focus on:

• The technology itself

• The level of autonomy

• The method of deployment

• The human control requirement

• The consequences of use

This disagreement remains one of the largest barriers to international consensus.

10. Verification and Enforcement Challenges

Even if international agreements are established, verifying compliance would be difficult. Unlike traditional weapons, AI systems are often software-based and can be modified rapidly.

A state could potentially claim that a system maintains human control while secretly developing greater autonomy.

Additionally, many AI technologies have dual-use applications, meaning the same technology can be used for civilian and military purposes.

Creating effective verification mechanisms without restricting legitimate technological innovation remains a major challenge.
