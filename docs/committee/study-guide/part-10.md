# PART V – PREVIOUS EFFORTS TO ADDRESS THE AGENDA BY COUNTRIES

Evolution of International and National Efforts to Regulate Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems and Military Artificial Intelligence

The international response toward Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS) and Military Artificial Intelligence has developed gradually as states, international organizations, researchers, and civil society actors have attempted to address the complex legal, ethical, strategic, and technological challenges created by autonomous warfare. Unlike traditional weapons regulation, where negotiations often focused on physical capabilities such as missiles, nuclear weapons, chemical agents, or conventional arms, the governance of LAWS presents unique difficulties because the central concern is not only the weapon itself but also the software, algorithms, data, and decision-making processes that enable autonomous action.

Previous efforts to address this issue have emerged through multiple pathways, including diplomatic negotiations under United Nations frameworks, national defence policies, military AI strategies, ethical guidelines, confidence-building measures, and initiatives by humanitarian organizations. However, despite decades of discussion, no universally accepted legally binding agreement specifically regulating LAWS has yet been established. The primary reason for this limited progress is the disagreement among states regarding whether autonomous weapons require a new international treaty or whether existing international humanitarian law is sufficient.

Early International Recognition of Autonomous Weapons Challenges

The emergence of autonomous weapons as an international security concern began receiving significant attention during the early twenty-first century as advances in robotics, unmanned aerial vehicles, artificial intelligence, and machine learning transformed military capabilities. Initially, autonomous systems were primarily associated with surveillance, reconnaissance, and logistics. However, rapid technological developments raised concerns that future weapons could independently identify and engage targets without direct human authorization.

Humanitarian organizations and academic researchers were among the first actors to highlight the potential consequences of autonomous warfare. The International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC) argued that autonomous weapons raise fundamental questions regarding compliance with international humanitarian law, particularly the principles of distinction and proportionality. The organization emphasized that warfare involves complex moral and legal judgments that may not be appropriately delegated to machines (ICRC, 2016).

Similarly, civil society campaigns such as the Campaign to Stop Killer Robots emerged to advocate stronger restrictions or complete prohibition of weapons capable of independently selecting and attacking targets. These initiatives argued that allowing machines to make life-and-death decisions could create accountability gaps and undermine human dignity.

United Nations Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW) Process

The most significant international diplomatic effort addressing autonomous weapons has occurred through the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW). Established in 1980, the CCW provides a framework for regulating weapons considered excessively injurious or indiscriminate.

In 2013, discussions regarding autonomous weapons began within the CCW framework following growing international concern about emerging military autonomy. States agreed to establish informal meetings of experts to examine the legal, military, ethical, and technological implications of LAWS.

The creation of the Group of Governmental Experts (GGE) on LAWS in 2016 represented a major step toward structured international dialogue. The GGE brought together representatives from governments, international organizations, and experts to discuss possible approaches toward regulating autonomous weapons.

The GGE process has produced several areas of general agreement among participating states. These include recognition that international humanitarian law applies to all weapons systems, the importance of human responsibility, the need for accountability, and the importance of maintaining international peace and security.

However, negotiations have remained difficult because states disagree regarding the appropriate level of regulation. Some countries advocate a legally binding treaty prohibiting certain autonomous weapons, while others support non-binding principles focused on responsible development and deployment.

Efforts by the United States

The United States has primarily addressed military AI through national defence policies rather than international restrictions. The Department of Defense has developed guidelines governing autonomous and semi-autonomous weapon systems through Directive 3000.09.

The policy requires appropriate levels of human judgment in the use of force, rigorous testing, legal review, and responsible command structures. The United States argues that these measures demonstrate its commitment to responsible military AI while preserving innovation.

Additionally, the United States has promoted international cooperation through initiatives encouraging responsible military AI development. In 2023, the United States introduced principles regarding responsible military AI use, emphasizing accountability, reliability, safety, transparency, and compliance with international law (U.S. Department of State, 2023).

However, Washington has consistently opposed international treaties that would prohibit autonomous weapons, arguing that such restrictions could prevent beneficial defensive applications and create strategic disadvantages.

Efforts by China

China has participated actively in international discussions through the CCW framework while promoting its own approach toward AI governance. Beijing has supported discussions on regulating autonomous weapons and has proposed principles emphasizing responsible use and preventing misuse.

China has argued that international governance should focus primarily on the use of autonomous weapons rather than restricting research and development. This distinction reflects China’s desire to maintain technological competitiveness while presenting itself as supportive of responsible governance.

China has also developed broader national strategies for artificial intelligence, including military modernization programmes integrating AI into defence capabilities. Through initiatives such as the New Generation Artificial Intelligence Development Plan, China has identified AI as a strategic technology central to future national power.

Efforts by Russia

Russia has approached autonomous weapons regulation primarily through the perspective of strategic stability and national security. Moscow has participated in international discussions but has generally opposed legally binding restrictions.

Russia argues that existing international humanitarian law is capable of regulating emerging military technologies and warns that additional restrictions may limit legitimate defence capabilities.

At the national level, Russia has invested in autonomous vehicles, robotics, unmanned systems, and AI-enabled military platforms. The country views AI as a critical component of future warfare and military modernization.

European Union and European State Efforts

European states have contributed significantly to efforts promoting ethical and responsible military AI governance. Unlike some major powers, many European countries have emphasized the importance of legal frameworks, transparency, and human control.

The European Union has developed broader AI governance principles emphasizing trustworthy artificial intelligence. Although defence applications remain outside much EU regulation due to national security considerations, these principles influence European approaches toward military AI. Countries including Germany, France, the Netherlands, Switzerland, and Austria have supported stronger international discussions on maintaining meaningful human control. Several European states have also supported diplomatic initiatives seeking clearer international norms regarding autonomous weapons.

Efforts by Middle Eastern and Asian Countries

Countries outside Western alliances have also contributed to discussions on autonomous weapons. India has advocated a balanced approach emphasizing responsible regulation while protecting technological development opportunities. Japan and South Korea have supported international principles promoting human oversight while continuing military modernization.

Middle Eastern states have taken diverse approaches. Israel, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE emphasize technological development and defence innovation, while also recognizing the importance of responsible use. Iran supports regulation but opposes frameworks that could restrict technological sovereignty.

Global South Initiatives

Developing countries have played an important role in emphasizing humanitarian concerns and equitable governance. Many Global South states argue that autonomous weapons could deepen existing global inequalities by concentrating military power among technologically advanced countries.

Countries such as Brazil, Mexico, South Africa, and several African and Asian states have supported stronger international regulation, transparency, and human accountability.

Mexico has been particularly active in advocating stronger restrictions, emphasizing that decisions involving lethal force should remain under human control. South Africa has similarly supported humanitarian approaches based on international law and ethical responsibility.

Civil Society and Expert Contributions

Beyond governments, civil society organizations, researchers, and technology experts have significantly influenced discussions on autonomous weapons. The Campaign to Stop Killer Robots, academic institutions, and humanitarian organizations have highlighted concerns regarding accountability, algorithmic bias, unpredictability, and civilian protection.

These actors have contributed research, policy recommendations, and public awareness campaigns, encouraging governments to establish stronger international norms.

However, critics argue that complete prohibition may be difficult to achieve because many autonomous technologies have legitimate civilian and defensive applications.
