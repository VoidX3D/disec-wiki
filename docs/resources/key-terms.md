# Key Terms & Definitions

44 essential terms for DISEC — Part II-B of the Study Guide.

| Term | Category | Definition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Artificial Intelligence (AI)** | technology | Computer systems designed to perform tasks that normally require human intelligence, including learning from data, recognising patterns, solving problems, understanding language, making predictions and supporting complex decision-making processes. |
| **Autonomy** | technology | The capability of a system to perform designated tasks independently after activation without requiring continuous human intervention. Unlike automated systems, autonomous systems perceive their environment, process information, adapt to changing conditions and select appropriate responses. |
| **Automation** | technology | Systems that execute predefined rules and programmed instructions repeatedly without possessing the ability to learn, adapt or independently make judgments. Examples include conventional missile guidance systems, automated radar tracking, and aircraft autopilot systems. |
| **Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS)** | weapons | Weapon systems capable of selecting and engaging targets with lethal force after activation without requiring further human intervention during the engagement process. The defining characteristic is the delegation of life-and-death decisions to machines with minimal or no real-time human control. |
| **Meaningful Human Control (MHC)** | legal | Ensuring that human operators retain sufficient involvement, understanding and authority over critical functions associated with the use of force. Requires that individuals possess adequate information regarding system capabilities, operational context, and potential consequences before authorising lethal actions. |
| **Machine Learning (ML)** | technology | Algorithms that identify patterns from extensive datasets and improve predictive performance through iterative training, rather than relying exclusively upon explicitly programmed instructions. Military applications include object recognition, facial identification, cyber defence, intelligence analysis, predictive logistics, autonomous navigation and decision-support systems. |
| **Human-in-the-Loop (HITL)** | control | Systems requiring explicit human authorisation before lethal force is employed, ensuring direct human responsibility for engagement decisions. |
| **Human-on-the-Loop (HOTL)** | control | Systems allowing autonomous functions to operate independently while maintaining continuous human supervision, enabling operators to intervene or abort missions if necessary. |
| **Human-out-of-the-Loop (HOOTL)** | control | Systems permitting machines to independently detect, identify, select, and engage targets without real-time human approval once activated. The most controversial category. |
| **Target Recognition** | technology | The technological capability of AI-enabled systems to identify and classify potential military objectives using computer vision, sensor fusion, radar signatures, thermal imaging, and behavioural analysis. |
| **Swarm Intelligence** | technology | The coordinated operation of multiple autonomous systems acting collectively without centralised control. Inspired by biological systems such as ant colonies, bee swarms and schools of fish. |
| **International Humanitarian Law (IHL)** | legal | Also known as the law of armed conflict. The principal legal framework governing the use of force during armed conflicts. Core principles: distinction, proportionality, military necessity, and precaution. |
| **Distinction** | legal | The principle requiring parties to an armed conflict to differentiate between combatants and civilians, targeting only legitimate military objectives. |
| **Proportionality** | legal | The principle prohibiting attacks expected to cause excessive civilian harm relative to the anticipated military advantage. |
| **Accountability Gap** | legal | The phenomenon in which responsibility for unlawful acts committed by autonomous weapons becomes diffused across multiple actors without a clearly identifiable decision-maker. |
| **Dual-Use Technology** | technology | Technologies that can be used for both civilian and military purposes. AI is inherently dual-use — the same algorithms that power civilian applications can be adapted for military use. |
| **Algorithmic Warfare** | military | A concept where AI assists commanders by processing enormous volumes of information faster than human operators, analysing satellite imagery, identifying potential threats, predicting equipment failures, and improving operational planning. |
| **Intelligentized Warfare** | military | Chinese military doctrine emphasizing the integration of AI, robotics, information systems, and autonomous technologies into future military operations. AI becomes a central factor in strategic competition. |
| **Network-Centric Warfare** | military | Military doctrine that emphasizes the importance of information dominance, technological superiority, and machine-assisted decision-making through interconnected networks of sensors, shooters, and command nodes. |
| **Confidence-Building Measures (CBMs)** | diplomatic | Transparency initiatives designed to reduce uncertainty, build trust, and prevent misunderstanding or accidental escalation between states. |
| **Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW)** | treaty | Adopted in 1980, the CCW seeks to prohibit or restrict weapons considered excessively injurious or indiscriminate. Primary international forum for autonomous weapons discussions. |
| **Group of Governmental Experts (GGE)** | diplomatic | Operating under the CCW framework, the GGE examines technological developments, ethical concerns, legal implications, and possible regulatory approaches to autonomous weapons. |
| **Verification** | diplomatic | The process of confirming compliance with international agreements. For AI systems, verification is particularly challenging because AI systems are often software-based and can be modified rapidly. |
| **Arms Race** | security | A competition between states to develop and deploy weapons systems faster than rivals, often without adequate consideration of safety or humanitarian consequences. |
| **Strategic Stability** | security | The condition in which no state has an incentive to initiate a first strike because the expected costs outweigh any potential benefits. |
| **Escalation** | security | The intensification of a conflict beyond its initial scope, often involving increased use of force, broader geographic involvement, or deployment of more destructive weapons. |
| **Proliferation** | security | The spread of weapons technologies to additional states or non-state actors. Unlike nuclear weapons, many AI-enabled systems rely on commercially available technologies. |
| **Edge Computing** | technology | Processing data near the source of data generation rather than in a centralized cloud, enabling faster response times for autonomous systems. |
| **Sensor Fusion** | technology | The process of combining data from multiple sensors (visual, radar, infrared, acoustic) to create a more complete and accurate picture of the operational environment. |
| **Loitering Munition** | weapons | A weapon system that combines characteristics of a drone and a missile, capable of circling an area for an extended period before attacking a target. |
| **Autonomous Underwater Vehicle (AUV)** | technology | An unmanned submersible vehicle capable of operating independently underwater without direct human control. |
| **ISR** | military | Intelligence, Surveillance, and Reconnaissance — the collection and analysis of information about an adversary's capabilities and intentions. |
| **Cyber Warfare** | military | The use of digital attacks to disrupt, damage, or destroy computer systems and networks of adversaries, often combined with autonomous systems. |
| **Asymmetric Warfare** | military | A conflict where opposing forces differ significantly in military power and rely on unconventional tactics to offset the advantages of a stronger adversary. |
| **Proxy Warfare** | military | A conflict where major powers support allied groups or states to advance their interests without engaging in direct military confrontation. |
| **Technological Sovereignty** | diplomatic | The ability of a state to develop, control, and maintain critical technologies without dependence on foreign suppliers. |
| **Preemptive Ban** | diplomatic | A prohibition established before a technology becomes widely deployed, aimed at preventing humanitarian harm before it occurs. |
| **Responsible Innovation** | diplomatic | An approach to research and development that considers ethical, social, and environmental implications throughout the innovation process. |
| **Export Controls** | diplomatic | Government regulations restricting the export of certain technologies, goods, or information for national security or foreign policy reasons. |
| **International Criminal Court (ICC)** | legal | A permanent international court established to prosecute individuals for genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, and aggression. |
| **Jus ad Bellum** | legal | The body of international law governing the conditions under which states may resort to war or use armed force in general. |
| **Jus in Bello** | legal | The body of international law governing the conduct of armed conflict (also known as International Humanitarian Law). |
| **Casus Belli** | legal | An act or event that provokes or justifies a war or military action. |
| **Status Quo Ante** | diplomatic | The existing state of affairs, especially the political or military situation before a conflict or change. |

## Context & Sources

### Artificial Intelligence (AI)

Military AI has transformed intelligence analysis, surveillance, logistics, cyber defence, command-and-control systems and decision-support mechanisms. However, military AI encompasses a much broader spectrum than autonomous weapons alone.

*Source: Russell and Norvig, 2021*

### Autonomy

The degree of autonomy exists along a spectrum ranging from low-level automation to full operational independence. In military operations, autonomy may involve navigation, obstacle avoidance, surveillance, target recognition, or target selection and engagement.

*Source: Scharre, 2018*

### Automation

Frequently confused with autonomy. Automated systems increase efficiency and precision but remain constrained by deterministic programming established by human operators.

*Source: UNIDIR, 2023*

### Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS)

Discussions within the CCW GGE consistently focus on systems that combine target detection, identification, selection and attack using autonomous functions. Distinguished from remotely piloted drones and conventional precision-guided munitions.

*Source: CCW GGE, 2024*

### Meaningful Human Control (MHC)

One of the central principles in international discussions. No universally accepted legal definition currently exists. Generally implies that humans remain capable of supervising, intervening and terminating autonomous operations.

*Source: Article 36, 2023*

### Machine Learning (ML)

Deep learning, a specialised subset based upon artificial neural networks, has significantly enhanced the ability of AI systems to process satellite imagery, sensor data and complex battlefield environments. May produce unpredictable outputs and inherit biases.

*Source: Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville, 2016*

### Human-in-the-Loop (HITL)

The most conservative model of human-machine interaction. Human operators make the final decision on target selection and engagement.

*Source: Scharre, 2018*

### Human-on-the-Loop (HOTL)

Middle ground between full human control and full autonomy. Human monitors the system and can override decisions.

*Source: Scharre, 2018*

### Human-out-of-the-Loop (HOOTL)

Raises profound concerns regarding accountability, ethical responsibility, civilian protection and compliance with International Humanitarian Law. Most states oppose this level of autonomy for lethal decisions.

*Source: Scharre, 2018*

### Target Recognition

No AI system currently demonstrates perfect reliability under all battlefield conditions. Environmental complexity, incomplete data, adversarial deception, and civilian presence may lead to erroneous classifications.

*Source: ICRC, 2021*

### Swarm Intelligence

Swarming technologies offer military advantages by increasing resilience, reducing operational costs and overwhelming conventional defensive systems. They also amplify concerns regarding escalation, attribution, command responsibility and civilian protection.

*Source: Various*

### International Humanitarian Law (IHL)

Whether fully autonomous weapons can consistently satisfy IHL obligations remains one of the most contentious legal questions confronting the international community.

*Source: Geneva Conventions, 1949*

### Distinction

Autonomous systems must possess sufficient technological capability to identify legitimate targets accurately. Current AI systems may struggle in complex environments where civilian and military objects are difficult to distinguish.

*Source: Protocol I, Additional to the Geneva Conventions*

### Proportionality

Unlike simple identification tasks, proportionality requires contextual judgment involving moral, strategic, and legal considerations. Raises questions regarding whether autonomous systems can perform such assessments reliably.

*Source: Protocol I, Additional to the Geneva Conventions*

### Accountability Gap

Traditional legal systems assign responsibility for unlawful acts to identifiable human actors. Autonomous weapons complicate this framework because decision-making may involve software developers, manufacturers, commanders, and operators simultaneously.

*Source: Human Rights Watch, 2020*

### Dual-Use Technology

The dual-use nature of AI complicates regulation, as restrictions affecting military AI may also influence commercial innovation, scientific research, and economic development.

*Source: Various*

### Algorithmic Warfare

The US military has embraced this concept. It provides significant advantages in speed, accuracy, and decision-making but raises concerns about reducing meaningful human oversight.

*Source: US DoD, 2023*

### Intelligentized Warfare

China's national AI development strategies and military modernization programmes emphasize intelligentized warfare as the future of conflict.

*Source: Kania, 2017*

### Network-Centric Warfare

AI enables militaries to process enormous amounts of information collected from satellites, drones, sensors, communication networks, and intelligence systems.

*Source: Various*

### Confidence-Building Measures (CBMs)

In the context of LAWS, CBMs could include sharing information about military AI development, testing procedures, safety mechanisms, and human oversight policies.

*Source: Various*

### Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW)

In 2013, discussions on autonomous weapons began within the CCW framework. The GGE on LAWS was established in 2016 as the primary diplomatic mechanism for examining autonomous weapons.

*Source: UN, 1980*

### Group of Governmental Experts (GGE)

The GGE has produced guiding principles including the responsibility of humans for decisions involving the use of force, compliance with IHL, and importance of accountability. Has not produced a legally binding agreement.

*Source: CCW, 2016*

### Verification

A state could potentially claim that a system maintains human control while secretly developing greater autonomy. Many AI technologies have dual-use applications.

*Source: Various*

### Arms Race

The development of military AI has created concerns regarding a new technological arms race. Major powers increasingly view artificial intelligence as a strategic advantage.

*Source: Various*

### Strategic Stability

Autonomous systems capable of operating at machine speed may compress decision-making timelines during crises, increasing the likelihood of miscalculation.

*Source: SIPRI, 2025*

### Escalation

AI-enabled early warning systems, autonomous cyber responses, and algorithm-driven command support may inadvertently amplify rather than reduce strategic instability.

*Source: Various*

### Proliferation

Terrorist organizations, criminal groups, or insurgent movements could potentially modify commercially available autonomous technologies for harmful purposes.

*Source: Various*

### Edge Computing

Edge computing allows autonomous weapons to operate in environments with limited or no communication connectivity, increasing their independence.

*Source: Various*

### Sensor Fusion

Sensor fusion is critical for autonomous systems to perceive and understand their environment. It enables target recognition and navigation in complex settings.

*Source: Various*

### Loitering Munition

Iran's Shahed-136 is one of the most widely used loitering munitions globally. These systems blur the line between drones and autonomous weapons.

*Source: Various*

### Autonomous Underwater Vehicle (AUV)

Naval forces are developing AUVs for long-duration surveillance, mine countermeasures, and anti-submarine warfare.

*Source: Various*

### ISR

AI has transformed ISR by enabling faster processing of satellite imagery, signals intelligence, and open-source data.

*Source: Various*

### Cyber Warfare

AI-enabled cyber operations can identify vulnerabilities, develop sophisticated attacks, and conduct information operations at machine speed.

*Source: Various*

### Asymmetric Warfare

Iran has embraced asymmetric warfare through its drone programme, demonstrating that relatively inexpensive autonomous systems can challenge technologically superior adversaries.

*Source: Various*

### Proxy Warfare

Iran's use of proxy forces equipped with autonomous systems represents a model of how LAWS can be deployed indirectly.

*Source: Various*

### Technological Sovereignty

Iran emphasizes technological sovereignty in the face of sanctions, arguing that developing states must have the right to develop indigenous AI capabilities.

*Source: Various*

### Preemptive Ban

Some states and organizations advocate for a preemptive ban on fully autonomous lethal weapons, similar to the Ottawa Treaty on landmines.

*Source: Various*

### Responsible Innovation

Many technologically advanced states support responsible innovation frameworks rather than outright prohibition of autonomous weapons.

*Source: Various*

### Export Controls

Controlling the export of autonomous weapons technologies is complicated by the dual-use nature of many AI components.

*Source: Various*

### International Criminal Court (ICC)

The ICC could potentially have jurisdiction over war crimes committed using autonomous weapons systems.

*Source: Rome Statute, 1998*

### Jus ad Bellum

Autonomous weapons raise questions about whether AI systems can make determinations about the legality of using force.

*Source: UN Charter*

### Jus in Bello

IHL principles of distinction, proportionality, and precaution must be applied to all weapons systems, including autonomous weapons.

*Source: Geneva Conventions*

### Casus Belli

The deployment of autonomous weapons by an adversary could be considered a casus belli, raising questions about preemptive responses.

*Source: International Law*

### Status Quo Ante

In negotiations, some states prefer maintaining the status quo ante regarding autonomous weapons development rather than imposing new restrictions.

*Source: Diplomacy*
